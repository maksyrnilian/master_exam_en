\section{Inertial frames, principles of relativity, the Galilean transformation, the Lorentz transformation and its consequences}
\label{sec:inertial-frames}

    An inertial frame is a frame of reference with respect to which every body, not subject to external interactions with other bodies, moves in uniform rectilinear motion or remains at rest. The existence of such a frame is postulated by Newton's first law of motion.

    The Galilean principle of relativity states that the laws of motion are identical in all inertial frames of reference, i.e.\ there is no privileged frame of reference. It was formulated in 1604 in the form of the following law: all frames of reference moving with respect to one another at constant velocity (including $\vec{v}=0$) are equivalent. Its consequence is the set of Galilean transformations between two frames of reference $(t,x,y,z)$ and $(t',x',y',z')$ with the primed one moving with velocity $v$ with respect to the unprimed one along the $x$ axis:

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:gallilean}
        \begin{aligned}
            t' &= t \\
            x' &= x - vt \\
            y' &= y \\
            z' &= z
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    Einstein additionally postulated that in every inertial frame of reference the speed of light has the same value. He made this assumption in 1905 based on an analysis of the transformations of Maxwell's equations, where he found that the electric and magnetic fields are not separately fixed: what one observer describes as a purely electric field, another observer moving with respect to the first describes as a mixture of an electric and a magnetic field. The two are aspects of one object rather than two independent fields.

    The Galilean transformations were thereby generalised to the Lorentz transformation to accommodate the Einstein's postulate:

    \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:lorentz}
        \begin{aligned}
            t' &= \gamma\left(t-xv/c^2\right) \\
            x' &= \gamma\left(x-tv\right) \\
            y' &= y \\
            z' &= z
        \end{aligned}
    \end{empheq}

    where the parameter $\gamma$ is known as the Lorentz factor:

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma}
        \gamma = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} \geq 1, \qquad \beta \equiv \frac{v}{c} .
    \end{equation}

    Note that in the limit $\nicefrac{v}{c} \to 0$ we have $\gamma \to 1$ and the Lorentz transformation reduces to the Galilean one (\cref{eq:gallilean}).

    \needspace{4\baselineskip}
    The consequences of the Lorentz transformation include:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Time dilation, described by the formula $\Delta t = \gamma\Delta t_0$, where $\Delta t_0$ is the \emph{proper time}---time recorded in the rest frame of the timed event, and $\Delta t$ is the time recorded by another observer moving with velocity $v$ with respect to the first. An interval is \emph{shortest} in the frame in which it is at rest.
        \item Lorentz contraction, described by the formula $L = \nicefrac{L_0}{\gamma}$, where $L_0$ is the \emph{proper length}---length of an object at rest, and $L$ is the length of the object recorded by an observer moving with respect to it at velocity $v$. An object is \emph{longest} in the frame in which it is at rest.
        \item Relativity of simultaneity: two events determined by one observer to be simultaneous need not be simultaneous for another observer. Simultaneity translates to other frames only for the events that happen at the same point in space.
        \item The description of physical quantities in four dimensions. Since \cref{eq:lorentz} mixes $t$ with $x$, a split into a \enquote{time part} and a \enquote{space part} is not something all observers agree on. Related quantities are therefore collected into \emph{four-vectors}, whose four components transform into one another exactly as position does,
        \begin{equation*}
            x^{\mu} =
            \begin{bmatrix}
                ct \\ x \\ y \\ z
            \end{bmatrix},
            \qquad
            p^{\mu} =
            \begin{bmatrix}
                E/c \\ p_x \\ p_y \\ p_z
            \end{bmatrix}.
        \end{equation*}
        Position is the prototype, and there the zeroth slot is literally time. What makes any other set of four quantities a four-vector is not an analogy with position but a transformation law. An equation written as an equality of four-vectors automatically holds in every inertial frame, because both sides transform in the same way. And each four-vector has one combination of its components that every observer agrees on: for position it is the spacetime interval $(c^{2}t^{2}-X^{2})$, and for momentum it is $(E^{2}-p^{2}c^{2})$.
    \end{enumerate}

    \paragraph{Mass--energy.}
        Writing out the invariant of the four-momentum gives the \emph{energy--momentum relation}
        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
        E^{2} = p^{2}c^{2} + m^{2}c^{4},
        \end{empheq}
        where $E$ is the total energy of a free body, $p$ the magnitude of its momentum, and $m$ its \emph{invariant mass}, meaning the mass measured in the frame in which the body is at rest. Two limits carry most of the physics. For a body at rest we have $p=0$ and the relation collapses to Einstein's $E=mc^{2}$, so that mass is itself a store of energy at the conversion rate $c^{2}\simeq\qty{9e16}{\joule\per\kilogram}$---the reason a milligram of mass defect suffices to power a nuclear reaction. For a massless particle such as the photon we have $m=0$ and the relation gives $E=pc$, a case $E=mc^{2}$ cannot describe at all. The invariant mass of a composite system also counts the internal energy of its parts, so a hot or compressed body is very slightly more massive than a cold relaxed one, and roughly \qty{99}{\percent} of a proton's mass is the kinetic energy of its quarks and the energy of the gluon field rather than the quark masses themselves.

\section{Fundamental interactions; carriers and range of interactions, charges and characteristic energy scales}
\label{sec:interactions}

    Every process in physics comes down to particles interacting, and every known interaction belongs to one of four families. In a quantum theory an interaction is not a force reaching across empty space but an \emph{exchange}: two particles interact by passing a third particle between them, called the \emph{interaction carrier}. The properties of the carrier---its mass, its spin, and the kind of charge it responds to---fix everything else, in particular how far the interaction reaches and how strong it is.

    What each interaction is responsible for:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Strong.} Binds quarks into protons, neutrons and other \emph{hadrons}, meaning particles built out of quarks. What is left over from this binding reaches slightly beyond each nucleon and holds protons and neutrons together in the nucleus. That residue is what overcomes the electrostatic repulsion between the protons and lets nuclei with more than one proton exist at all.
        \item \textbf{Electromagnetic.} Binds electrons to nuclei, atoms into molecules and molecules into solids, so all of chemistry and all of ordinary matter's rigidity. Light is a propagating disturbance of the same field.
        \item \textbf{Weak.} Converts one kind of quark or lepton into another. It drives beta decay, the first step of hydrogen fusion in stars, and essentially every interaction a neutrino ever has.
        \item \textbf{Gravitational.} Acts on everything that carries energy. It is by far the feeblest between two individual particles, but it dominates on astronomical scales because its charge---mass--energy---comes with one sign only and never cancels.
    \end{itemize}

    The first three are described by the \emph{Standard Model}, the quantum field theory of elementary particles. Matter in it consists of twelve spin-\nicefrac{1}{2} particles arranged in three generations: six quarks (up and down, charm and strange, top and bottom) and six leptons (electron, muon and tau, each with its neutrino). Ordinary matter uses only the first generation---up and down quarks and the electron---while the heavier generations are copies that decay quickly. The interactions are carried by spin-1 bosons: eight gluons, the photon, and the $W^{+}$, $W^{-}$ and $Z^{0}$ bosons. One further particle, the spin-0 \emph{Higgs boson} of mass about $125$~GeV, is the quantum of the field that gives the $W$, the $Z$ and the charged fermions their masses. Gravitation stands outside this framework and has no accepted quantum description.

    A particle feels an interaction only if it carries the matching charge, which is what the third column lists. Colour charge comes in three values conventionally called red, green and blue, and is carried by quarks and gluons alone. Weak isospin is the charge of the weak interaction and is carried by all quarks and leptons.\footnote{More precisely by their left-handed components, which is why the $W$ couples only to those.} Its name records a mathematical analogy---it obeys the same algebra as spin---but it is not intrinsic angular momentum and has nothing to do with rotation in space. It sits in the charge column because that is the role it plays: it is the label deciding whether a particle couples to the interaction at all. A neutrino has neither electric charge nor colour, which is why it interacts only weakly and passes through the Earth almost undisturbed. Carriers may themselves be charged: each gluon carries one colour and one anticolour, so gluons attract one another, whereas the photon is electrically neutral and ignores other photons. This self-attraction of the gluon field is the reason the strong interaction behaves so differently from the other three.

    Energies are quoted in \emph{electronvolts}, $1$~eV being the energy an electron gains across a potential difference of one volt, $1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}$~J, with the multiples keV, MeV and GeV. Masses are quoted in the same units by \emph{mass--energy equivalence}, $E=mc^{2}$, a mass being named by the rest energy it corresponds to. Strictly the unit is then eV$/c^{2}$, but the $c^{2}$ is left out and one simply says the $W$ boson has a mass of $80$~GeV. In SI units $1$~eV$/c^{2}=1.783\times10^{-36}$~kg.

    The ranges in \cref{tab:interactions} are not independent facts but follow from the carrier masses. The energy--time uncertainty relation $\Delta E\,\Delta t\geq\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}$ says that a state existing only briefly cannot have a sharply defined energy. Note what this is about: the energy spread of a short-lived state itself, not the precision of our instruments and not the mass of the particle species, which is a fixed number measured on real particles in accelerators. An exchanged carrier is never observed directly and so need not balance the energy books---it is \emph{virtual}, in effect borrowed from the vacuum provided the loan is repaid quickly. Borrowing at least the rest energy $mc^{2}$ limits its life to $\Delta t\sim\nicefrac{\hbar}{mc^{2}}$, and travelling at most at the speed of light it covers

    \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:yukawa-range}
        R \sim c\,\Delta t \sim \frac{\hbar}{mc},
    \end{empheq}

    the Compton wavelength of the carrier. A heavy carrier therefore gives a short-ranged interaction and a massless one an unlimited range, which is why electromagnetism and gravitation reach as far as they do. Using $\hbar c\approx197$~MeV\,fm with a carrier rest energy of $80$~GeV gives $R\approx2.5\times10^{-18}$~m for the weak interaction, the order of magnitude quoted in \cref{tab:interactions}.

    The strong interaction is the apparent exception, since its carrier is massless and yet it does not reach beyond the nucleus. Its range is set instead by \emph{confinement}: because gluons attract one another, colour charge can never be isolated, and gluons do not travel freely between well-separated particles. What acts between nucleons is a residual effect carried by \emph{pions}, the lightest hadrons, of rest energy about $140$~MeV, and \cref{eq:yukawa-range} then returns the familiar $1.4\times10^{-15}$~m.

    Beta decay shows the pieces working together. A down quark inside a neutron turns into an up quark and emits a $W^{-}$, which decays at once into an electron and an electron antineutrino, so that overall $n\to p+e^{-}+\bar{\nu}_{e}$. The energy released is a few MeV, thousands of times less than the $80$~GeV needed to make a real $W$. The boson can therefore only ever be virtual, and it is this mismatch of scales---not any small coupling---that makes the weak interaction weak and slow at everyday energies.

    Collecting these, each interaction has its own characteristic energy scale:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Electromagnetic.} Atomic and molecular binding, from about $1$~eV for a chemical bond to $13.6$~eV to ionise hydrogen, rising to keV for inner electrons of heavy atoms.
        \item \textbf{Strong.} Nuclear binding of roughly $8$~MeV per nucleon, hadron masses of order $1$~GeV, and an underlying scale of a few hundred MeV at which the interaction becomes strong.
        \item \textbf{Weak.} Set by the carrier masses, of order $100$~GeV, even though the energy actually released in weak decays is only of order MeV.
        \item \textbf{Gravitational.} No scale at all in everyday physics, since the interaction has no intrinsic energy of its own. Quantum effects are expected only at the \emph{Planck energy}, about $10^{19}$~GeV, which is beyond any conceivable accelerator.
    \end{itemize}

\section{Conservation laws in physics and their connection with symmetry laws}
\label{sec:conservation}

    The \emph{symmetries} of a physical system are closely connected with its \emph{conservation laws}. A symmetry here means that the system goes on behaving in exactly the same way after some transformation has been applied to it---shifting the whole experiment in time, moving it to another place, turning it to face another direction.

    Noether's theorem formalises the connection: to every continuous symmetry of the action there corresponds a conserved quantity. \emph{Continuous} means the transformation can be made arbitrarily small, as a rotation can but a mirror reflection cannot.

    \subsection{The Lagrangian, the action and the equation of motion}

        The \emph{Lagrangian} of a system is the difference between its kinetic and its potential energy,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:lagrangian}
            \Lagr \equiv T-V,
        \end{equation}

        and the \emph{action} is the Lagrangian accumulated along a path in time,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:action}
            S \equiv \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \Lagr \, dt .
        \end{equation}

        $S$ is not a number belonging to the system but a number belonging to a whole candidate path, one value for every path one might consider. An object that eats a function and returns a number in this way is called a \emph{functional} (\cref{sec:variational}).

        Demanding that $S$ be \emph{stationary}---unchanged to first order when the path is deformed slightly with its two endpoints held fixed---singles out the path actually followed and yields the \emph{Euler--Lagrange equation},

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:euler-lagr}
            \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{q}} \right) = \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial q},
        \end{empheq}

        where $q$ is any coordinate used to specify the configuration of the system and $\dot{q} = \nicefrac{dq}{dt}$. This is the principle of least action (\cref{sec:variational}) in one line, and for a single particle in a potential it returns Newton's second law (\cref{eq:newton2}).

        The two derivatives\footnote{
        A \emph{partial derivative} $\partial f/\partial x$ is the rate of change of $f$ with respect to $x$ when every other argument of $f$ is held fixed, so its value depends entirely on which variables those are --- thermodynamics makes this explicit by writing the held quantities as subscripts, as in $(\partial U/\partial V)_{T}$. A \emph{total derivative} $\mathrm{d}f/\mathrm{d}t$ asks a different question: how $f$ changes along an actual path, counting every route by which $t$ influences it. The \emph{chain rule} connects the two,
        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
            \frac{\mathrm{d}f}{\mathrm{d}t} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \sum_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{\mathrm{d}q_{i}}{\mathrm{d}t},
        \end{empheq}
        for a function $f(q_{1},\dots,q_{n},t)$ whose arguments $q_{i}$ themselves vary with $t$. The first term is the \emph{explicit} dependence on $t$ --- what changes because the rule defining $f$ was written with a $t$ in it --- and the sum is the \emph{implicit} dependence, what changes because the system has moved. This distinction is the whole content of several standard results.
        } in \cref{eq:euler-lagr} do different jobs. The Lagrangian is treated as a function of three independent slots, $\Lagr(q,\dot{q},t)$, so the partial derivative $\nicefrac{\partial}{\partial q}$ varies $q$ alone and holds $\dot{q}$ and $t$ fixed. The total derivative $\nicefrac{d}{dt}$ is taken after an actual trajectory has been substituted, where $q$ and $\dot{q}$ are themselves functions of time, so by the chain rule it collects every dependence on $t$, the implicit ones as well as the explicit one. 

    \subsection{Noether's theorem}

        \textit{If the action of a system is invariant under a continuous transformation, then there is a quantity built from the state of the system that does not change in time.}

        The theorem is stated for the action rather than for the equations of motion because the action is what those equations follow from, so a symmetry of $S$ is automatically a symmetry of the dynamics. In practice the symmetry is tested on $\Lagr$ instead, which is easier: $S$ is only $\Lagr$ integrated over time, so a transformation leaving $\Lagr$ unchanged leaves $S$ unchanged too.\footnote{The converse fails. $\Lagr$ may change by the total time derivative of some function and still leave $S$ stationary on the same paths, so invariance of $\Lagr$ is sufficient but not necessary.} Each example below therefore begins by asking which variable is missing from the Lagrangian.


    \subsection{Examples of the application of Noether's theorem}

        \subsubsection{Conservation of energy}

            Symmetry under a shift in time (homogeneity of time) leads to the law of conservation of energy. Suppose the Lagrangian does not depend explicitly on time, meaning that repeating the experiment tomorrow changes nothing,

            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial t} = 0 .
            \end{equation*}

            Differentiating $\Lagr$ along the trajectory and substituting \cref{eq:euler-lagr} then gives $\nicefrac{d}{dt}\left(\dot{q}\,\nicefrac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{q}} - \Lagr\right) = -\nicefrac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial t} = 0$, so that

            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{q}}\,\dot{q} - \Lagr = \const = \Ham,
            \end{equation*}

            which defines the \emph{Hamiltonian}. Whenever the kinetic energy is quadratic in the velocities and the coordinates carry no explicit time dependence---the ordinary case---this evaluates to

            \begin{equation}\label{eq:hamiltonian}
                \Ham \equiv T + V,
            \end{equation}

            the total energy of the system.


        \subsubsection{Conservation of momentum}

            Spatial symmetry (homogeneity of space) leads to the law of conservation of momentum. If the Lagrangian does not depend explicitly on position,

            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \vec{r}} = 0,
            \end{equation*}

            then inserting this into \cref{eq:euler-lagr} leads to

            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{\vec{r}}} \right) = 0.
            \end{equation*}

            With $ \vec{p} \equiv \nicefrac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{\vec{r}}} $, we have

            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} = 0 \, \Rightarrow \, \vec{p}=\const,
            \end{equation*}

            so that the momentum is conserved.

            For contrast, take a particle of mass $m$ in a uniform gravitational field, for which
            \begin{equation*}
                \Lagr = \tfrac{1}{2}m\dot{\vec{r}}^{\,2} - mgz .
            \end{equation*}
            This does depend on position, through $z$: moving the whole experiment upwards changes the potential energy, so space is not homogeneous in that direction. The vertical momentum is accordingly not conserved and the particle accelerates downwards. The horizontal coordinates $x$ and $y$ still do not appear, so $p_x$ and $p_y$ are conserved. A conservation law follows from a symmetry only in the directions in which that symmetry actually holds.

        \subsubsection{Conservation of angular momentum}

            Symmetry under rotation (isotropy of space) leads to the law of conservation of angular momentum. Turning the whole system through a small angle $\delta\vec{\phi}$ shifts every position by $\delta\vec{r} = \delta\vec{\phi} \times \vec{r}$ and every velocity by $\delta\dot{\vec{r}} = \delta\vec{\phi} \times \dot{\vec{r}}$. Requiring the Lagrangian not to notice,

            \begin{equation*}
                \delta\Lagr = \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \vec{r}} \cdot \delta\vec{r} + \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{\vec{r}}} \cdot \delta\dot{\vec{r}} = 0,
            \end{equation*}

            and substituting \cref{eq:euler-lagr} together with $\vec{p} \equiv \nicefrac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{\vec{r}}}$ collapses this to

            \begin{equation*}
                \delta\vec{\phi} \cdot \frac{d}{dt}\left( \vec{r} \times \vec{p} \right) = 0 .
            \end{equation*}

            The axis $\delta\vec{\phi}$ was arbitrary, so the bracket itself must be constant and the angular momentum is conserved,

            \begin{equation*}
                \vec{L} \equiv \vec{r} \times \vec{p} = \const .
            \end{equation*}

            In words: if turning the apparatus to face a different direction makes no difference to how it behaves, the combination $\vec{r} \times \vec{p}$ cannot change with time.\todoper{Watch Allan Adams on it.}

\section{Newton's laws of motion and the limits of their applicability; classical mechanics as a limiting form of quantum mechanics; non-relativistic classical mechanics as the limit of relativistic classical mechanics}
\label{sec:newton}

    \subsection{Newton's laws of motion}

    Newton's laws of motion are three laws lying at the foundation of classical mechanics, specifying the relations between the motion of a body and the forces acting on it, which is why they are also called the laws of motion. In quantum mechanics they are for the most part inapplicable, and in relativistic mechanics they hold only within a limited range.

    \paragraph{Newton's first law}

        In an inertial frame of reference (\cref{sec:inertial-frames}), if no force acts on a body, or if the acting forces balance one another, then the body remains at rest or moves in uniform rectilinear motion.

        The first law assumes the existence of an inertial frame of reference but does not indicate how such a frame is to be sought, and it is therefore a postulate of the existence of an inertial frame of reference---\textit{there exists a frame of reference in which a particle, not subject to interaction with its surroundings, remains at rest or moves along a straight line with constant velocity.}

    \paragraph{Newton's second law}

        If an unbalanced force acts on a body, then the body moves with an acceleration directly proportional to the force and inversely proportional to the mass of the body.

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:newton2}
            \vec{F} = m \vec{a}.
        \end{equation}

    \paragraph{Newton's third law}

        Interactions between bodies are always mutual. In an inertial frame of reference the forces of mutual interaction of two bodies have the same magnitude, opposite directions, and different points of application (each acts on a different body).

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:newton3}
            \vec{F}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21}
        \end{equation}

        In classical mechanics, Newton's third and second laws combined are equivalent with the law of conservation of momentum.

    \subsection{Limits of applicability of Newton's laws}

        Newton's laws of motion lose their direct validity in non-inertial frames, where it becomes necessary to introduce fictitious forces (sourceless, not subject to the 3rd law). Newtonian mechanics also becomes more complicated in more intricate coordinate systems. In such cases the Lagrangian or Hamiltonian formalisms, based on the principle of least action (\cref{sec:variational}), work better.

        Quantum mechanics describes the behaviour of particles on small scale, where  Newtonian mechanics fails. For example, the uncertainty principle (\cref{sec:uncertainty}) tells us that we cannot simultaneously arbitrarily well know the position and the momentum of a particle, whereas classical mechanics rests \emph{on their exact trajectories}.

        The results of quantum mechanics in the limit of large quantum numbers should generally agree with the results of classical mechanics (Bohr's correspondence principle). In particular, assuming $\hbar \to 0$ may be regarded as a transition from a discrete to a continuous system. Some phenomena, however, have no classical limit at all: the stability of the atom and the discreteness of its spectrum (\cref{sec:quantisation}), tunnelling through a barrier (\cref{sec:tunnelling}), and the exclusion of identical fermions from a common state (\cref{eq:identical}), which is what gives matter its volume and the periodic table its structure.

        Moreover, Newton assumed that time is absolute, which was only countered 300 years later by Einstein and his theory of relativity, where the Galilean transformation is replaced by the Lorentz transformation (\cref{sec:inertial-frames}) and the two agree only to the extent that $\gamma \to 1$ for $v \ll c$ (\cref{eq:gamma}). For this reason classical mechanics still remains a useful tool for describing non-relativistic phenomena. Again, certain effects are inherently relativistic even though they are directly observable: cosmic-ray muons reach sea level in far greater numbers than their lifetime would allow without time dilation, and the atomic clocks aboard GPS satellites have to be corrected for both special- and general-relativistic effects or the fix drifts by kilometres a day.

\section{The postulates of quantum mechanics; measurement, state of a physical system}
\label{sec:postulates}

    Quantum mechanics rests on a handful of \emph{postulates}---statements that are not derived from anything deeper, but assumed, and then justified by the fact that everything they predict turns out to be correct. They answer three questions: what a state \emph{is}, what a measurement \emph{does}, and how a state \emph{changes} in time.

    \subsection{The wave function}

        Classically, to know a particle is to know its position and momentum at every instant. Quantum mechanics replaces both by a single complex-valued function of position and time, the \emph{wave function} $\Psi(x,t)$.

        \begin{postulate}{the state}
            \label{post:state}
            The state of a system is completely described by a wave function $\Psi(x,t)$. Everything that can be known about the system is contained in it.
        \end{postulate}

        The wave function is not itself observable. Experiments return real numbers, and $\Psi$ is complex, so it cannot be measured. What can be measured is its squared modulus $|\Psi|^{2} = \Psi^{*}\Psi$, which is real and never negative.

        \begin{postulate}{the Born rule}
            \label{post:born}
            $|\Psi(x,t)|^{2}$ is a \emph{probability density}: the probability of finding the particle between $a$ and $b$ at time $t$ is
            \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:born-position}
                P_{a \le x \le b}(t) = \int^{b}_{a}|\Psi(x,t)|^{2}\,dx .
            \end{empheq}
        \end{postulate}

        Because the particle must be somewhere, the wave function is \emph{normalised},

        \begin{equation*}
            \int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|\Psi(x,t)|^{2}\,dx = 1 .
        \end{equation*}

        This forces two requirements that do real work later. $\Psi$ must be \emph{single-valued}, meaning one value of $\Psi$ at each $(x,t)$, and it must die away fast enough at large $|x|$ for the integral to converge. Note also that multiplying $\Psi$ by a constant phase $e^{i\alpha}$ changes no probability, so two states differing only by such a \emph{global phase} are physically the same state.

    \subsection{Superposition}

        \begin{postulate}{superposition}
            \label{post:superposition}
            Any sum of possible states is itself a possible state: if $\Psi_{1}$ and $\Psi_{2}$ are states, so is $c_{1}\Psi_{1} + c_{2}\Psi_{2}$ with complex $c_{1}, c_{2}$.
        \end{postulate}

        If a system can be prepared in state A and in state B, it can be prepared in the combination \enquote{A + B}, realising both alternatives at once. The equation governing $\Psi$ turns out to be linear (\cref{eq:tdse}), so each term evolves on its own and the system stays a superposition at every later instant.

    \subsection{Why the wave function is complex: interference}

        A complex number whose squared modulus gives a probability is called a \emph{probability amplitude}. It carries a magnitude \emph{and} a phase, and the phase is what separates quantum from classical probability. Probabilities are positive and cannot cancel. Amplitudes can. This is forced on the theory rather than chosen for convenience: the $i$ sitting on the left of \cref{eq:tdse} means $\Psi$ cannot be a real function, and without a phase there would be nothing to cancel and no interference at all.

        Two rules govern them. Amplitudes for steps taken \emph{in succession} multiply. Amplitudes for \emph{alternative} routes to the same outcome add, provided nothing in the experiment records which route was taken. The squared modulus is taken only at the very end.

        In the double slit, let $A_{1}$ and $A_{2}$ be the amplitudes for a photon to reach a chosen point of the screen through slit 1 and slit 2. With both slits open and no record of which was used, the alternatives are \emph{indistinguishable}, the amplitudes add, and

        \begin{equation*}
            P = |A_{1} + A_{2}|^{2} = |A_{1}|^{2} + |A_{2}|^{2} + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left(A_{1}^{*}A_{2}\right).
        \end{equation*}

        The last term is the \emph{interference term}. It depends on the relative phase of the two amplitudes, which shifts across the screen as the path difference changes, and it is what produces the fringes. Add a detector that records the slit and the alternatives become distinguishable, so it is now the probabilities that add, $P = |A_{1}|^{2} + |A_{2}|^{2}$, with no interference and no fringes. Amplitudes add when the alternatives are not distinguished, probabilities add when they are. The information that would tell them apart is called \emph{which-path information}, and merely having it available in the apparatus is enough to destroy the fringes.

    \subsection{Observables as operators}

        Position probabilities come straight out of \cref{eq:born-position}. Every other measurable quantity has to be extracted from $\Psi$, and the tool for that is an \emph{operator}: an instruction that takes one function and returns another. Position acts by multiplication and momentum by differentiation,

        \begin{equation*}
            \hat{x}\,\Psi = x\,\Psi, \qquad
            \hat{p} = -i\hbar\nabla, \qquad
            \hat{p}_{x} = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x} .
        \end{equation*}

        The momentum operator is read off from the de Broglie relation $p = \hbar k$ (\cref{eq:de-broglie}): differentiating a plane wave $e^{ikx}$ brings down a factor $ik$, and $ik \cdot (-i\hbar) = \hbar k$. The operator of the total energy, the \emph{Hamiltonian}, is then built from it exactly as the classical $\Ham = T + V$ (\cref{eq:hamiltonian}) is,

        \begin{equation*}
            \hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m} + V(x) = -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2} + V(x) ,
        \end{equation*}

        where

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:nabla}
        \nabla \equiv
            \begin{bmatrix}
                \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \\[1ex] \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \\[1ex] \frac{\partial}{\partial z}
            \end{bmatrix}.
        \end{equation}

        \begin{postulate}{observables}
            \label{post:observables}
            To every measurable quantity $q$ there corresponds a \emph{Hermitian} operator $\hat{Q}$ acting on the wave function. Hermitian means $\hat{Q} = \hat{Q}^{\dag}$, where $\dag$ denotes transposition combined with complex conjugation.
        \end{postulate}

        The restriction is not arbitrary. Hermitian operators have real eigenvalues, as measured numbers must be, and their eigenfunctions belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal and together form a complete set. The possible outcomes are therefore mutually exclusive and exhaust the possibilities.

    \subsection{Eigenstates and measurement}

        An operator normally changes the function it acts on. The exceptional case is when it merely rescales it,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:eigenvalue}
            \hat{Q}\,\psi_{n} = q_{n}\,\psi_{n},
        \end{equation}

        where $\psi_{n}$ is an \emph{eigenfunction} of $\hat{Q}$ and the number $q_{n}$ its \emph{eigenvalue}. These are the states in which the observable has a definite value, and they are the exception rather than the rule: almost every state \emph{is not} an eigenfunction of a given operator.

        Because the eigenfunctions form a complete set, any state can be written as a sum of them,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:expansion}
            \Psi = \sum_{n}c_{n}\,\psi_{n}, \qquad c_{n} \in \mathbb{C},
        \end{equation}

        and because they are \emph{orthonormal}, $\int\psi_{n}^{*}\psi_{m}\,dx = \delta_{nm}$ (the Kronecker delta, $1$ when $n=m$ and $0$ otherwise), each coefficient is recovered by $c_{n} = \int\psi_{n}^{*}\Psi\,dx$.

        \begin{postulate}{measurement}
            \label{post:measurement}
            The only possible result of measuring $\hat{Q}$ is one of its eigenvalues $q_{n}$, obtained with probability $|c_{n}|^{2}$. Immediately after a measurement returning $q_{n}$, the system is in the corresponding eigenstate $\psi_{n}$.
        \end{postulate}

        The Born rule of \cref{eq:born-position} is the special case of this for position. The final sentence is the \emph{collapse} of the wave function, and it is the one step in the theory that is neither smooth nor reversible: a state carrying a whole range of possible answers becomes, abruptly, a single eigenfunction. Why this happens is unexplained and is called the \emph{measurement problem}. If several eigenfunctions share one eigenvalue (a \emph{degenerate} outcome), the measurement leaves behind only the part of the state built from those particular eigenfunctions, renormalised, rather than one definite eigenfunction.

        Which eigenfunctions you expand in depends on what you intend to measure. The eigenfunctions of $\hat{H}$ are not those of $\hat{p}$. The state is one object, written down in whichever set matches the apparatus, much like the same vector written in different bases.

        Applying \cref{eq:eigenvalue} to the energy gives the \emph{time-independent Schrödinger equation},

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:tise}
            \hat{H}\,\psi(x) = E\,\psi(x) .
        \end{empheq}

        Despite the name, this is not a wave equation---there is no time in it. It is an energy eigenvalue equation, and its solutions are the states of definite energy.

    \subsection{Where quantisation comes from}
    \label{sec:quantisation}

        Quantisation is \emph{not} a postulate. It falls out of \cref{eq:tise} once the conditions on $\Psi$ are imposed: the wave function must be finite, single-valued, normalisable and continuous, and its first derivative must be continuous as well wherever the potential is finite\footnote{Where the potential is infinite there is no tunnelling, the wave function is forced to zero at the boundary, and its derivative is allowed to jump there.}.

        When the particle is \emph{bound}, meaning trapped in a potential well, these conditions can be met only for particular values of $E$, and the energy spectrum is discrete. An unbound particle is not boxed in by them and its spectrum is continuous. The mechanism is the one that gives a guitar string fixed at both ends its discrete set of frequencies. The difference, and it is not a small one, is that here it is the \emph{energy} that is discrete, which classical physics never predicts.

        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Infinite square well} of width $L$: $E_{n} = \nicefrac{n^{2}\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}{2mL^{2}}$. Tighter confinement means larger spacing. The lowest state has $E_{1} \neq 0$, the \emph{zero-point energy}: a confined quantum particle can never be completely at rest.
            \item \textbf{Harmonic potential} $V \propto x^{2}$: evenly spaced levels $E_{n} = \left(n + \nicefrac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega$, with gap $\hbar\omega$. Any potential minimum looks harmonic for small displacements, so this is the generic case for vibrations of molecules and solids.
            \item \textbf{Hydrogen atom}: the confining agent is the electrostatic attraction of the nucleus, $V(r) = -\nicefrac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r}$, so the field of the nucleus is what does the confining. The bound states ($E<0$) form the discrete series $E_{n} = -\nicefrac{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}}{n^{2}}$, crowding together as $n$ grows because the well is shallower further out. Above $E = 0$ the electron is free and the spectrum is continuous.
            \item \textbf{Angular momentum} is quantised for a related but distinct reason: the wave function must return to itself after a full turn, which forces $L_{z} = m\hbar$ with integer $m$. This is confinement in angle rather than in radius.
            \item \textbf{Free particle}: confined by nothing, so neither its energy nor its momentum is quantised.
        \end{itemize}

        Light is also exchanged in quanta, $E = h\nu = \hbar\omega$. This does \emph{not} follow from the postulates above, which describe a fixed set of particles and say nothing about the electromagnetic field. It is an experimental input, and it is recovered as a result only once the field itself is treated quantum-mechanically.

    \subsection{Time evolution}

        \begin{postulate}{time evolution}
            \label{post:evolution}
            Between measurements the state evolves according to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation,
            \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:tdse}
                i\hbar\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t} = \hat{H}\Psi .
            \end{empheq}
        \end{postulate}

        The Schrödinger equation is a guess. It is not derived from anything deeper, and the reasoning behind it runs backwards, from the answer to the equation. Start from a de Broglie wave, $\Psi \propto e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$, and from two relations already supplied by experiment, $E = \hbar\omega$ and $p = \hbar k$. What we want to reproduce is the ordinary classical statement $E = \nicefrac{p^{2}}{2m} + V$, so look at the powers in it: energy appears once, momentum appears squared. We therefore need something that pulls \emph{one} factor of $\omega$ out of the wave, and something that pulls \emph{two} factors of $k$ out of it.

        Differentiation does precisely that, because each derivative of an exponential brings its exponent down as a multiplying factor. Dressed with the right constants,

        \begin{align*}
            i\hbar\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t} &= i\hbar\left(-i\omega\right)\Psi = \hbar\omega\,\Psi = E\,\Psi , \\[4pt]
            -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2} \Psi}{\partial x^{2}} &= -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(ik\right)^{2}\Psi = \frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m}\,\Psi = \frac{p^{2}}{2m}\,\Psi .
        \end{align*}

        This is the point of the exercise. Each combination hands back \emph{the same wave multiplied by the number we were after}, which is exactly the eigenvalue pattern of \cref{eq:eigenvalue}: differentiation is the machinery that extracts a physical number from a wave. Substituting these two expressions for $E$ and $\nicefrac{p^{2}}{2m}$ in the classical energy statement gives \cref{eq:tdse}. The equation is, in effect, reverse-engineered---the simplest linear equation whose plane-wave solutions carry the right energy and the right momentum. It is not a fundamental law in the way Maxwell's equations are. It is kept because everything it predicts turns out to be right.

        Because the equation is \emph{first} order in time, unlike a classical wave equation, the state at one instant fixes it at every other. The evolution keeps the total probability equal to $1$ and is fully reversible, and evolution of that kind is called \emph{unitary}. It stands in sharp contrast to the abrupt, irreversible collapse of the measurement postulate, and the tension between the two \emph{is} the measurement problem.

        If the Hamiltonian does not itself depend on time, \cref{eq:tdse} separates into a space part and a time part. The space part is \cref{eq:tise}, and the full solution differs from it only by a rotating phase,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:stationary}
            \Psi(x,t) = \psi(x)\,e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}Et} .
        \end{equation}

        This fixes the convention used throughout the note: capital $\Psi$ or $\Phi$ is the full, time-dependent wave function, lower-case $\psi$ or $\phi$ the time-independent part alone. Such a state is called \emph{stationary}, because the phase cancels in every squared modulus and so no probability changes with time. The phase turns at angular frequency $\omega = \nicefrac{E}{\hbar}$, so $E = \hbar\omega$ appears here as a property of the state, not as a statement about photons.

        On its own a rotating phase does nothing. In a superposition it does a great deal. Take a state built from two stationary states of different energy,

        \begin{equation*}
            \Psi = c_{1}\psi_{1}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}E_{1}t} + c_{2}\psi_{2}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}E_{2}t} .
        \end{equation*}

        The two phases turn at different rates, so the phase \emph{difference} between the terms grows steadily, and the interference term in $|\Psi|^{2}$ oscillates at the frequency $\omega_{21} = \nicefrac{\left(E_{2}-E_{1}\right)}{\hbar}$. The probability density sloshes back and forth at that frequency instead of standing still, and for an electron in an atom $\omega_{21}$ is precisely the frequency of the light emitted or absorbed on that transition. A \emph{global} phase changes nothing, a \emph{relative} phase changes everything.

    \subsection{States as vectors: Dirac notation}

        \Cref{eq:expansion} fixes the state entirely by the list of coefficients $c_{n}$, and a list of numbers is a vector. This is the starting point of Heisenberg's \emph{matrix mechanics}: wave functions are vectors, and the eigenfunctions are the basis vectors of the space they live in.

        That space is a \emph{Hilbert space} $\Hilb$: a complex vector space equipped with a scalar product, of finite or infinite dimension. The abstract vector is written $\ket{\Psi}$ and called a \emph{ket}, so that \cref{eq:expansion} becomes $\ket{\Psi} = \sum_{n}c_{n}\ket{\psi_{n}}$. In a basis, $\ket{\Psi}$ is a column of complex numbers, and the \emph{bra} $\bra{\Psi} = \ket{\Psi}^{\dag}$ is the corresponding row with every entry conjugated.\footnote{The two possible orders of multiplication give quite different objects. Row times column, $\braket{\Phi|\Psi} = \begin{bmatrix} b_{1}^{*} & b_{2}^{*} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} c_{1} \\ c_{2} \end{bmatrix} = b_{1}^{*}c_{1} + b_{2}^{*}c_{2}$, is $1\times2$ times $2\times1$, so the result is $1\times1$: a single number. Column times row, $\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Phi} = \begin{bmatrix} c_{1} \\ c_{2} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} b_{1}^{*} & b_{2}^{*} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c_{1}b_{1}^{*} & c_{1}b_{2}^{*} \\ c_{2}b_{1}^{*} & c_{2}b_{2}^{*} \end{bmatrix}$, is $2\times1$ times $1\times2$, so the result is $2\times2$: a matrix, that is, an operator.}

        The scalar product is inherited from the overlap integral of two wave functions,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:inner-product}
            \braket{\Phi|\Psi} = \int \Phi^{*}(x)\,\Psi(x)\,dx = \sum_{n}b_{n}^{*}c_{n} \in \mathbb{C},
        \end{equation}

        where $b_{n}$ and $c_{n}$ are the expansion coefficients of the two states. The conjugation makes it Hermitian rather than symmetric, $\braket{\Phi|\Psi} = \braket{\Psi|\Phi}^{*}$, so that $\braket{\Psi|\Psi} \ge 0$ is real and its square root is the length of the vector. Orthonormality reads $\braket{\psi_{n}|\psi_{m}} = \delta_{nm}$ with the Kronecker delta as before, the coefficients are recovered as $c_{n} = \braket{\psi_{n}|\Psi}$, and the measurement postulate takes its compact form,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:born-amplitude}
            P_{n} = \left|\braket{\psi_{n}|\Psi}\right|^{2}.
        \end{equation}

        In this language an operator on an $m$-dimensional space is an $m \times m$ matrix, Hermiticity is the statement that the matrix equals its own conjugate transpose, and \cref{eq:eigenvalue} is an ordinary matrix eigenvalue problem.

    \subsection{The simplest example: spin}

        Some degrees of freedom have no wave function in position at all, and for these the vector language is the only one available. Electron spin is the smallest case, a two-dimensional Hilbert space (spin itself is treated in \cref{sec:angular-momentum}). Take the states $\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow}$, spin up and down along $z$, as the basis:

        \begin{equation*}
            \ket{\uparrow} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
            \ket{\downarrow} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
            \hat{S}_{z} = \frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}.
        \end{equation*}

        Multiplying out gives $\hat{S}_{z}\ket{\uparrow} = +\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\hat{S}_{z}\ket{\downarrow} = -\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}\ket{\downarrow}$, so the basis states are eigenvectors and the only possible results are $\pm\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}$. Now take the superposition

        \begin{equation*}
            \ket{\Psi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\uparrow} + \ket{\downarrow}\right)
            = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
        \end{equation*}

        The amplitude for spin up is $\braket{\uparrow|\Psi} = \nicefrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, so by \cref{eq:born-amplitude} the probability is $\nicefrac{1}{2}$, and likewise for spin down. Acting with $\hat{S}_{z}$ gives $\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}\cdot\nicefrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\uparrow}-\ket{\downarrow}\right)$, which is not a multiple of $\ket{\Psi}$: the operator has genuinely rotated the vector, as it does for any state that is not one of its eigenvectors. The same $\ket{\Psi}$ is nonetheless an exact eigenvector of $\hat{S}_{x} = \nicefrac{\hbar}{2}\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ with eigenvalue $+\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}$. Having a sharp $x$-component costs it all knowledge of the $z$-component, which is the uncertainty principle (\cref{sec:uncertainty}) in its simplest form.

    \subsection{Mean values}

        Ordinary probability theory defines a mean in two equivalent ways. For a quantity with discrete possible values $q_{n}$ occurring with probabilities $P_{n}$, the mean is $\sum_{n}q_{n}P_{n}$. For a continuous variable with normalised probability density $P(x)$, it is $\ev{x} = \int xP(x)\,dx$.

        Quantum mechanics uses exactly these, with $P_{n} = |c_{n}|^{2}$. A single measurement returns one eigenvalue. Repeating it on many identically prepared systems gives a distribution whose mean is

        \begin{equation*}
            \ev{\hat{Q}} = \sum_{n}q_{n}|c_{n}|^{2} = \bra{\Psi}\hat{Q}\ket{\Psi} = \int \Psi^{*}(x)\,\hat{Q}\,\Psi(x)\,dx .
        \end{equation*}

        The forms agree because the operator can be rebuilt from its own outcomes and eigenstates,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:spectral}
            \hat{Q} = \sum_{n}q_{n}\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}},
        \end{equation}

        where each $\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}}$ is a \emph{projection operator}, a matrix that discards everything in a state except its $\ket{\psi_{n}}$ component. (\Cref{eq:spectral} as written assumes a discrete, non-degenerate spectrum.) The mean is generally \emph{not} one of the possible outcomes: for the spin state above $\ev{\hat{S}_{z}} = 0$, a value never seen in any single run.

    \subsection{Systems with several degrees of freedom}

        If a system has more than one degree of freedom (a photon carries both a position and a polarisation), each degree of freedom has its own Hilbert space $\Hilb_{i}$.

        \begin{postulate}{composite systems}
            \label{post:composite}
            The Hilbert space of a composite system is the \emph{tensor product} of the spaces of its parts, $\Hilb = \bigotimes_{i}\Hilb_{i}$.\footnote{The tensor product $\Hilb_{1} \otimes \Hilb_{2}$ is the space whose basis vectors are all the pairs formed by taking one basis vector from $\Hilb_{1}$ and one from $\Hilb_{2}$. Its dimensions therefore multiply rather than add: a two-state spin combined with a three-state degree of freedom gives a six-dimensional space, not a five-dimensional one.}
        \end{postulate}

        A state that cannot be written as a single such pair is called \emph{entangled}. Take two spins in

        \begin{equation*}
            \ket{\Psi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\uparrow\uparrow} + \ket{\downarrow\downarrow}\right),
        \end{equation*}

        where $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ means the first spin up and the second down. No choice of a state for the first spin and a state for the second reproduces this, so neither particle has a state of its own---only the pair does.

        It follows from what is already above rather than from anything new. By the superposition postulate the pair may sit in a combination of the two joint alternatives, and by the measurement postulate the collapse applies to the pair as a whole. Measuring the first spin gives up or down with probability $\nicefrac{1}{2}$ each, and whichever comes out, the state collapses to a single term and the second spin is then certain to match, however far apart the two have been carried. This is why the tensor product is more than bookkeeping: most states in the product space are not products at all.

\section{Angular momentum and the phenomenon of precession --- classical and quantum description}
\label{sec:angular-momentum}

    The classical angular momentum of a system is defined as

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:momentum}
        \vec{L} \equiv \sum_i \vec{r}_i \times \vec{p}_i,
    \end{equation}

    where $\vec{r}_i$ is the position and $\vec{p}_i$ the momentum of the $i$-th element of the system. It is the quantity conserved when space is isotropic (\cref{sec:conservation}).

    In quantum mechanics the same expression is promoted to an operator (\cref{post:observables}), the \emph{orbital} angular momentum

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:orbital-angular-momentum}
        \hat{L} = -i\hbar\left(\vec{r}\times \nabla\right).
    \end{equation}

    A particle also carries \emph{spin}, an angular momentum that is not associated with any motion through space. It transforms under rotations exactly as $\hat{L}$ does, but it has no classical counterpart and is not a picture of the particle turning about itself. Like $\hat{L}$ it is a vector of three operators, one observable per axis,

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:spin-angular-momentum}
        \hat{S} \equiv
            \begin{bmatrix}
                \hat{S}_{x} \\ \hat{S}_{y} \\ \hat{S}_{z}
            \end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation}

    and for an electron each component is a $2\times2$ matrix. This is the operator already used as the worked example in \cref{sec:postulates}, where $\hat{S}_{z}$ was written out, its eigenvectors $\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow}$ identified, and a measurement on a superposition of them followed through. That discussion is the concrete content of \cref{eq:spin-angular-momentum} and is not repeated here.

    The total angular momentum is the sum of the two,

    \begin{equation*}
        \hat{J} = \hat{L} + \hat{S} .
    \end{equation*}

    \subsection{Quantisation and quantum numbers}

        Only two things about an angular momentum can be known at once: the magnitude of its square, and one chosen component, conventionally the one along $z$. The other two components then have no definite value. Both of the knowable quantities are quantised,

        \begin{equation*}
            \hat{L}^{2}\ket{l,m} = \hbar^{2}l(l+1)\ket{l,m}, \qquad
            \hat{L}_{z}\ket{l,m} = \hbar m\ket{l,m},
        \end{equation*}

        with $l = 0,1,2,\dots$ and $m = -l,-l+1,\dots,+l$, so an angular momentum of a given magnitude has $2l+1$ allowed orientations. The same pattern holds for $\hat{S}$ with the pair $(s,m_s)$ and for $\hat{J}$ with $(j,m_j)$, except that $s$ and $j$ are allowed to be half-integer. An electron always has $s = \nicefrac{1}{2}$ and therefore $m_s = \pm\nicefrac{1}{2}$, which is where its two states come from.

        For one electron in a hydrogen atom the numbers divide the work between them.\todo{Not sure if Hydrogen here fits bets but some section should discuss quantum numbers and fine/hyperfine structure. Avoid repetition. Keep it in one place and refer to it from elsewhere.}

        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Principal, $n = 1,2,3,\dots$} Fixes the energy, $E_{n} = -\nicefrac{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}}{n^{2}}$ (\cref{sec:quantisation}), and roughly the size of the orbital.
            \item \textbf{Orbital, $l = 0,\dots,n-1$} Fixes how much orbital angular momentum the electron has, $|\vec{L}| = \hbar\sqrt{l(l+1)}$, and hence the shape of the orbital. These are the labels s, p, d, f.
            \item \textbf{Magnetic, $m = -l,\dots,+l$} Fixes the tilt of that angular momentum, its projection on a chosen axis. Nothing distinguishes the $2l+1$ values until a magnetic field selects a direction, at which point the level splits into $2l+1$ (the Zeeman effect).
            \item \textbf{Spin, $m_{s} = \pm\nicefrac{1}{2}$} The projection of the spin, doubling every count above.
        \end{itemize}

        Since $\vec{L}$ and $\vec{S}$ belong to the same electron, they combine into $\vec{J}$, and $j$ then runs from $|l-s|$ to $l+s$ in integer steps---for $l=1$ and $s=\nicefrac{1}{2}$, either $j=\nicefrac{1}{2}$ or $j=\nicefrac{3}{2}$. The two combinations differ slightly in energy because the electron's magnetic moment sits in the magnetic field produced by its own orbital motion, and that splitting is the \emph{fine structure} of the spectrum.


    \subsection{Classical precession}

        Precession is the slow circular drift of the axis of a spinning object. Instead of pointing in a fixed direction, the spin axis itself sweeps around, tracing out the surface of a cone. The familiar example is a spinning toy top: as it spins, its axis does not simply fall over under gravity but slowly circles around the vertical.

        The angular momentum $\vec{L}$ of the spinning body points along its spin axis, and a torque changes it at the rate $\nicefrac{d\vec{L}}{dt} = \vec{\tau}$. If the torque is perpendicular to $\vec{L}$, then so is the increment it adds, so it turns $\vec{L}$ without lengthening it and the axis sweeps out a cone. For the toy top, gravity acting at the centre of mass supplies the torque, and unless the axis stands exactly vertical that torque is horizontal and therefore perpendicular to $\vec{L}$. It seems counterintuitive that the spin axis does not tilt in the direction of the force but it \emph{should not}---it will tilt exactly in the direction of the supplied torque of the force.

        The precession frequency is given by

        \begin{equation*}
            \omega_p = \frac{\tau}{I_s\omega_s\sin(\theta)}
        \end{equation*}

        where $\tau$ is the torque, $I_s$ the moment of inertia, $\omega_s$ the spin angular velocity, and $\theta$ the angle between the spin axis and the vertical axis about which it precesses.

    \subsection{Quantum precession}
    \label{subsec:quantum-precession}

        A particle such as an electron carries a magnetic moment $\vec{\mu}$ associated with its spin angular momentum $\vec{S}$---the two are proportional, $\vec{\mu} = \gamma\vec{S}$ with $\gamma$ the \emph{gyromagnetic ratio}, and so lie along the same axis. Placed in an external magnetic field $\vec{B}$, the moment feels a torque

        \begin{equation*}
            \vec{\tau} = \vec{\mu}\times \vec{B},
        \end{equation*}

        which is perpendicular to $\vec{S}$ and therefore, exactly as in the classical case, makes $\vec{S}$ sweep around the direction of $\vec{B}$ instead of aligning with it. This is Larmor precession, the direct analogue of gravity making a toy top precess, with the spin angular momentum $\vec{S}$ playing the role of the top's angular momentum $\vec{L}$.

        The rate of this precession, the \emph{Larmor frequency} $\omega_{L} = \gamma B$, depends only on the field strength and not at all on how far $\vec{S}$ is tilted away from $\vec{B}$, because the two factors of $\sin\theta$ that enter the torque and the cone radius cancel. Every spin in a sample therefore turns at the same rate whatever its orientation, which is what makes the effect usable for magnetic resonance.\todoper{watch youtube about it}

\section{Variational principles of physics; examples of equations that can be derived from them}
\label{sec:variational}

    \subsection{What a variational principle is}

        There are two complementary ways to describe how a system moves. Newton's is \emph{local}: at each instant the force fixes the acceleration, the acceleration updates the velocity, and the trajectory is assembled step by step. It requires every force to be known as a vector and resolved along fixed axes, forces of constraint included. A variational principle takes the opposite, \emph{global} view. It considers every path the system could conceivably follow between a fixed start point and a fixed end point, attaches a number to each, and asserts that the path actually taken is the one at which that number is stationary. It needs only the two scalars $T$ and $V$, in whatever coordinates suit the problem, and forces of constraint never appear.

        That number is the \emph{action} $S$ (\cref{eq:action}), which can be evaluated for any candidate path and not only for the true one. A quantity that takes in a whole function and returns a single number is called a \emph{functional}, as opposed to an ordinary function, which takes in a number and returns a number.

        The \emph{principle of least action} says that the path followed is the one for which the action is \emph{stationary}: deform the path slightly in any way, holding the endpoints fixed, and to first order the action does not change, written $\delta S = 0$. This is the same condition as $f'(x) = 0$ at the minimum of an ordinary function, with the single variable $x$ replaced by an entire path. Only in the simplest cases is the stationary path a true minimum, so \enquote{least} is something of a historical misnomer---a maximum or a saddle satisfies the condition equally well. 

        The idea began in optics as \emph{Fermat's principle}: light travels between two points along the route taking the least time, from which the laws of reflection and refraction follow (\cref{eq:snell}). The machinery came from a different kind of problem, the \emph{brachistochrone} (Johann Bernoulli, 1696): of all ramps joining a point to a lower one, find the shape down which a bead released from rest slides in the shortest time. The answer is a cycloid, the curve traced by a point on the rim of a rolling wheel. That is an optimisation posed by the experimenter rather than a law of nature---the bead does not choose its own ramp---so it is not itself an instance of the principle of least action. What the two share is the mathematics: in each, a whole curve is singled out by requiring an accumulated integral to be stationary. Problems of that shape are what the \emph{calculus of variations} of Euler and Lagrange was built for, and it is in that language that the principle of least action is written.\todoper{Check the example online. Maybe skip this historical nuance here.}

    \subsection{Equations that follow from it}

        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Euler--Lagrange equation.} Imposing $\delta S = 0$ on the mechanical action---the Lagrangian (\cref{eq:lagrangian}) integrated over time---converts the loose instruction \enquote{choose the best path} into a definite differential equation the path must satisfy at every instant (\cref{eq:euler-lagr}). For a single particle it reproduces Newton's second law (\cref{eq:newton2}), but now in whatever coordinates are convenient. These \emph{generalised coordinates} $q_i$ are any set of numbers that fixes the configuration of the system, such as an angle or a distance along a track, and the freedom to choose them is what makes the method far easier than Newton's for constrained or awkwardly shaped systems.
            \item \textbf{Hamilton's equations.} A standard change of variables (a \emph{Legendre transformation}) trades the velocities $\dot{q}_i$ for momenta and repackages the same content as the \emph{Hamiltonian} formulation, built on the total energy (\cref{eq:hamiltonian}). This is the usual starting point for statistical physics and for quantum mechanics.
            \item \textbf{Hamilton--Jacobi equation.} Treating the action as a function of the point at which the path ends turns the problem into a single equation for the action itself (here called Hamilton's principal function), in which every momentum is replaced by a derivative of the action, $p_i = \nicefrac{\partial S}{\partial q_i}$. Solving that one equation is an alternative route to the motion, and its form is strikingly close to the Schrödinger equation (\cref{eq:tdse}), which is why it is regarded as the classical doorway to wave mechanics.
            \item \textbf{Feynman's path integral.} The fully quantum version of the whole scheme. Instead of selecting one path it gives every path a contribution $e^{iS/\hbar}$ and adds them all together. Where $S$ is large compared with $\hbar$, neighbouring paths arrive with wildly different phases and cancel, except close to the stationary path where the phase barely varies---so the classical principle is recovered as a limit rather than assumed.
            \item \textbf{Maxwell's equations.} The recipe is not restricted to mechanics: write down an action for the system and demand that it be stationary. The action of the electromagnetic field, together with its coupling to charges, yields Maxwell's equations (\cref{eq:maxwell-general}).
            \item \textbf{Einstein's field equations.} The same demand applied to the Einstein--Hilbert action, whose integrand measures the curvature of spacetime, yields the field equations of general relativity. That one rule generates the governing equations of mechanics, electromagnetism and gravitation alike is what makes the variational viewpoint one of the unifying ideas of physics.
        \end{itemize}


\section{The laws of thermodynamics}
\label{sec:thermodynamics}

    \emph{Thermal equilibrium} implies there is no net flow of thermal energy between two systems that can exchange heat. A system is said to be in thermal equilibrium with itself if the temperature within the system is spatially uniform and temporally constant.

    \subsection{The zeroth law of thermodynamics}

        If systems A and B, capable of exchanging heat with one another, are in thermal equilibrium with each other, and the same is true of systems B and C, then systems A and C are also in thermal equilibrium with each other.

    \subsection{The first law of thermodynamics}

        The change in the internal energy of a system is equal to the sum of the heat supplied and the \emph{work done on the system}.

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:first-law}
            \Delta U = Q + W,
        \end{equation}

        or in a differential form:

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:first-law-differential}
            dU = dQ - p\, dV,
        \end{equation}

        where $dV$ is the change in volume of the system (positive when the system expands---it does work and loses internal energy, $W<0$).

    \subsection{The second law of thermodynamics}

        \begin{itemize}
            \item Clausius statement: \textit{There is no thermodynamic process whose sole result would be the absorption of heat from a reservoir at a lower temperature and its transfer to a reservoir at a higher temperature.}
            \item Kelvin statement: \textit{No process is possible whose sole effect would be the absorption of a certain quantity of heat from a reservoir and its conversion into an equivalent amount of work.}
            \item Planck statement: \textit{It is impossible to construct an engine which will work in a complete cycle, and produce no effect except the production of work and cooling of a heat reservoir.}
        \end{itemize}

        These statements are made quantitative by the \emph{Clausius inequality}, which holds for any process:

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:clausius-inequality}
            dS \geq \frac{\delta Q}{T}.
        \end{equation}

        Here $S$ is the \emph{entropy} and $T$ the temperature at which the heat $\delta Q$ crosses the boundary. Entropy is a \emph{function of state}, meaning its change between two equilibrium states depends only on those two states and not on the path taken between them, and that is what makes the left-hand side usable even though no instrument reads entropy directly. To obtain $\Delta S$ for a real process, however violent, one connects the same two end states by \emph{any} convenient reversible path and integrates $\nicefrac{\delta Q}{T}$ along that one instead, which is ordinary calorimetry. \Cref{eq:clausius-inequality} then measures the real process against its reversible ideal: the entropy actually gained is never less than the heat transfer accounts for, and the excess is entropy generated by the irreversibility itself. This is what caps the efficiency of every heat engine and fixes which way a process is allowed to run. The equality holds only for reversible processes. For an isolated system ($\delta Q = 0$) it reduces to

        \begin{equation*}
            dS \geq 0,
        \end{equation*}

        so spontaneous processes drive the system towards higher entropy and equilibrium is the state of maximum entropy. This macroscopic, thermodynamic entropy coincides with the microscopic count of accessible microstates (\cref{eq:boltzmann-entropy}), which is why a system left to itself drifts towards the macrostate realised by the overwhelmingly largest number of microstates.

        A process is \emph{reversible} if it can be run backwards through the same sequence of equilibrium states leaving no net change in either the system or its surroundings---an idealisation that requires it to be infinitely slow (quasi-static) and free of friction or other dissipation. Every real process is \emph{irreversible}: it generates entropy, so the inequality in \cref{eq:clausius-inequality} is then strict.

    \subsection{The third law of thermodynamics}

        The Nernst heat theorem: \textit{It is not possible, by means of a finite number of steps, to attain the temperature of absolute zero (zero kelvin) if we take a non-zero absolute temperature as our starting point.}

\section{Mechanical properties of material media: liquids, elastic media, mechanical waves}
\label{sec:mechanical-media}

    \paragraph{Liquids}

    Liquids are substances intermediate between a gas and a solid, having an easily variable shape but little compressibility. The properties of a liquid follow from the behaviour of its molecules:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item they have complete freedom of movement within the volume occupied by the liquid
        \item \emph{intermolecular interactions} act between them---the residual electrostatic attractions between whole neutral molecules--and within the bulk of the liquid these pull in every direction equally and cancel
        \item the intermolecular interactions do not cancel at the interface between the liquid and another phase, as a result of which the phenomenon of surface tension occurs.
    \end{itemize}

    \paragraph{Elastic media}

    An elastic medium is one in which a deformation gives rise to internal stresses that \emph{oppose} it, so that the body returns to its original shape once the external force is removed. \emph{Elastic} in the strict sense requires only that the deformation be fully recovered, not that the restoring force be proportional to it. Proportionality---the linear regime $F = -kx$, in which one may speak of an elastic modulus---is an extra assumption, though a safe one for small deformations: a system in stable equilibrium sits at a minimum of its potential energy, any smooth minimum looks like a parabola close up, and a parabolic potential gives a force linear in the displacement. Small disturbances therefore meet a linear elastic response whatever the medium does under large ones.

    Solids stay elastic over a wide range of stresses. Fluids---liquids and gases alike---always resist a change of \emph{volume}, so they possess bulk elasticity, but a fluid at rest offers no resistance whatever to a change of \emph{shape}, having no static shear elasticity, which is precisely what allows it to flow. Compressing the air in a cylinder closed by a piston raises its pressure and it pushes back: that is bulk elasticity, and it is all that a mechanical wave requires.\footnote{Air in a balloon is the more familiar illustration but a worse one, since the rubber skin is itself elastic and contributes a restoring force of its own, which obscures where the elasticity is coming from.} A fluid in the open shows an apparent \emph{shape} elasticity only under fast forcing, when it has no time to flow out of the way.

    \paragraph{Mechanical waves}

    Bulk elasticity is what carries a mechanical wave. Displace a small region of an elastic medium from its equilibrium position and it oscillates about that position, and because the medium is elastic the oscillation is passed on to neighbouring regions, so the disturbance travels through the medium as a whole. The speed of propagation is set by the medium alone, going roughly as the square root of its stiffness divided by its density. Waves are classified by the direction in which the medium oscillates relative to the direction of travel, which is also the direction in which energy is carried.

    A wave is \emph{longitudinal} when the medium oscillates parallel to the direction of propagation. Sound in air is the standard example, as is a spring alternately compressed and extended. A gas or a liquid can carry only this kind, since it has no shear elasticity to resist a sideways displacement.

    A wave is \emph{transverse} when the medium oscillates perpendicular to the direction of propagation. Examples are a plucked string and a vibrating ruler. Transverse mechanical waves need shear elasticity and so travel through solids only, which is how seismology distinguishes the Earth's liquid outer core from the solid mantle.

\section{Flow of an incompressible liquid; Bernoulli's principle}
\label{sec:bernoulli}

    Let us begin by defining the assumptions of the problem. It is an example from the field of ideal-fluid hydrodynamics, and therefore adopts certain simplifications:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item the fluid is incompressible, that is, it does not change its volume under pressure, which for liquids is usually a very good approximation
        \item friction arising from the viscosity of the liquid is negligible
        \item the flow of the liquid is steady and irrotational: at any fixed point in the pipe the velocity, density and so on do not change with time, and the fluid elements are carried along without spinning about themselves
    \end{itemize}

    Applying the principle of conservation of energy to the flow problem, one obtains Bernoulli's principle, which is usually written as:

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:bernoulli}
        \frac{v^2}{2} + gh + \frac{p}{\rho} = \const
    \end{equation}

    where we have, in turn, the kinetic energy, the potential energy and the energy due to pressure, each per unit mass of fluid. The constant keeps the same value all along a \emph{streamline}, meaning the path traced out by a small parcel of fluid as it is carried by the flow, and because the flow was assumed irrotational the same constant in fact serves the whole flow. The relation permits the description of flow through a pipe that changes its cross-section or its height, and it explains the so-called hydrodynamic paradox. Where a pipe narrows, incompressibility forces the liquid to flow faster, since the same volume must pass each second through a smaller area, so by \cref{eq:bernoulli} the larger $\nicefrac{v^2}{2}$ must be offset by a smaller pressure $p$. The counter-intuitive result is that the pressure is \emph{lower} where the pipe is narrow and the flow is fast.

\section{Maxwell's equations, the scalar and vector potentials of the electromagnetic field, the equations of the electromagnetic field in vacuum and in material media}

    \subsection{Maxwell's equations --- general form}

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:maxwell-general}
            \begin{alignedat}{2}
                \text{Gauss's law for } \vec{E}                                 & : \quad & \nabla \cdot \vec{E}  & = \frac{\rho_{\text{f}} + \rho_{\text{b}}}{\varepsilon_{0}}\\
                \text{Gauss's law for } \vec{B}                                 & : \quad & \nabla \cdot \vec{B}  & = 0\\
                \text{Faraday's law}                                            & : \quad & \nabla \times \vec{E} & = -\frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t}\\
                \text{Ampère--Maxwell law}                                      & : \quad & \nabla \times \vec{B} & = \mu_{0}\left(\vec{J_{\text{f}}} + \vec{J_{\text{b}}} + \varepsilon_{0}\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t}\right),
            \end{alignedat}
        \end{equation}

        where $\rho$ is a charge density, $\vec{J}$ a current density, $\vec{E}$ the electric field and $\vec{B}$ the magnetic induction field. Charges and currents are of two kinds. \emph{Free} ones ($\rho_{\text{f}}$, $\vec{J}_{\text{f}}$) are the ones we put there and control, such as the conduction electrons in a wire or the charge deposited on a plate. \emph{Bound} ones ($\rho_{\text{b}}$, $\vec{J}_{\text{b}}$) are locked inside the atoms of a material and can only shift or circulate slightly without leaving them, and it is the applied field itself that induces them. The fields make no such distinction---only the total charge and current act as sources---but it is worth making, because the bound part is a response of the material and can be absorbed into redefined fields (\cref{sec:maxwell-matter}).\todoper{Check Gritthits on bound currents}

        The nabla operator $\nabla$ (\cref{eq:nabla}) can be used to mean different operations, giving:

            \begin{aligned}
                \nabla \cdot \vec{A} &\text{ --- divergence of a vector field}\\
                \nabla \times \vec{A} &\text{ --- curl of a vector field}\\
                \nabla f &\text{ --- gradient of a scalar field}
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation*}

        Divergence measures how strongly a given point acts as a source or a sink of a vector field, curl measures the circulation of the field around a point, and the gradient points in the direction in which a scalar field increases fastest. Two integral theorems turn these statements about single points into statements about whole regions. The \emph{divergence theorem}, $\int_{V}\nabla\cdot\vec{A}\,dV = \oint_{\partial V}\vec{A}\cdot d\vec{S}$, says that the divergence added up over a volume equals the flux out through the surface enclosing it. \emph{Stokes' theorem}, $\int_{S}(\nabla\times\vec{A})\cdot d\vec{S} = \oint_{\partial S}\vec{A}\cdot d\vec{l}$, says that the curl added up over a surface equals the circulation of the field around the loop bounding it.     \subsection{Maxwell's equations in vacuum}

        In vacuum there are no charges or currents at all, free or bound, so $\rho = 0$ and $\vec{J} = 0$, and Maxwell's equations reduce to their source-free form:

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:maxwell-vacuum}
            \begin{aligned}
                \nabla \cdot \vec{E}  & = 0\\
                \nabla \cdot \vec{B}  & = 0\\
                \nabla \times \vec{E} & = -\frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t}\\
                \nabla \times \vec{B} & = \mu_{0}\varepsilon_{0}\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t}
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation}

    \subsection{Maxwell's equations in matter}
    \label{sec:maxwell-matter}

        An electric field pulls the positive and negative charge of each atom or molecule slightly apart, giving it an electric dipole moment $\vec{p} = q\vec{d}$, where $\vec{d}$ points from the negative charge to the positive one. Averaged per unit volume this is the polarisation vector

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:polarisation-vector}
            \vec{P}=\chi \varepsilon_0\vec{E},
        \end{equation}

        where $\chi$ --- electric susceptibility of a material. Polarisation moves bound charge only, and

        \begin{equation*}
            \nabla\cdot\vec{P}=-\rho_{\text{b}}.
        \end{equation*}

        The minus sign records which charge is left behind. Since $\vec{P}$ points from the negative end of each dipole to the positive end, a region out of which $\vec{P}$ spreads (positive divergence) is one from which positive bound charge has been carried away, so what remains there is negative.

        From Gauss's law for the electric field, we then have

        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{aligned}
                \nabla\cdot(\varepsilon_0\vec{E})          &= \rho_{\text{f}} + \rho_{\text{b}} \\
                \nabla\cdot(\varepsilon_0\vec{E}+\vec{P})  &= \rho_{\text{f}}, \\
                \nabla\cdot\vec{D} &= \rho_{\text{f}},
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation*}

        where $\vec{D}$ --- the electric displacement field,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:D-def}
            \vec{D} \equiv \varepsilon_{0}\vec{E} + \vec{P} .
        \end{equation}

        Adding $\vec{P}$ cancels the bound charge contributions, so $\vec{D}$ is the field whose sources are the free charges alone---the ones we control. $\vec{P}$ points in the opposite direction to the field it is producing, so it subtracts from $\vec{E}$.

        Matter responds magnetically as well. Every atom carries a small circulating current and so a magnetic dipole moment, and the average moment per unit volume is the \emph{magnetisation} $\vec{M}$. Inside a uniformly magnetised body, neighbouring loops run in opposite directions where they meet and cancel, so a net current survives only at the surface, or anywhere $\vec{M}$ varies from place to place. Added up, what is left is $\nabla\times\vec{M}$. A polarisation that changes in time is on top of that a genuine motion of bound charge, hence a current $\nicefrac{\partial\vec{P}}{\partial t}$. The two together make the bound current

        \begin{equation*}
            \vec{J}_{\text{b}} = \nabla\times\vec{M} + \frac{\partial\vec{P}}{\partial t}.
        \end{equation*}

        From the Ampère--Maxwell law, we then have

        \begin{equation*}
            \begin{aligned}
                \nabla\times\vec{B}                                     & = \mu_{0}\left(\vec{J_{\text{f}}} + \vec{J_{\text{b}}} + \varepsilon_{0}\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t}\right) \\
                                                                        & = \mu_{0}\left(\vec{J_{\text{f}}} + \nabla\times\vec{M} + \frac{\partial\vec{P}}{\partial t} + \varepsilon_{0}\frac{\partial\vec{E}}{\partial t}\right) \\
                \nabla\times\vec{H}  & = \vec{J_{\text{f}}} + \frac{\partial \vec{D}}{\partial t},
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation*}

        where $\vec{H}$ --- the magnetic field intensity,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:H-def}
            \vec{H} \equiv \frac{\vec{B}}{\mu_{0}} - \vec{M} .
        \end{equation}

        The minus here does the same job as the plus in \cref{eq:D-def}. Moving $\nabla\times\vec{M}$ to the left-hand side of the equation above is what subtracts $\vec{M}$, and it removes the bound magnetisation current from the curl, leaving $\vec{H}$ sourced by free currents alone. Both $\vec{D}$ and $\vec{H}$ are therefore devices for hiding the material's own response inside a redefined field, so that only the sources we control appear explicitly.

        \needspace{4\baselineskip}
        Maxwell's equation in matter may then finally be rewritten as:

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:maxwell-media}
            \begin{aligned}
                \nabla \cdot \vec{D} &= \rho_{\text{f}}\\
                \nabla \cdot \vec{B} &= 0\\
                \nabla\times\vec{E} &= -\frac{\partial \vec{B}}{\partial t}\\
                \nabla\times\vec{H} &= \vec{J}_{\text{f}} + \frac{\partial\vec{D}}{\partial t}
            \end{aligned}
        \end{equation}

    \subsection{The scalar and vector potentials}\label{sec:em-potentitals}

        Two of Maxwell's equations carry no sources at all, and they can be satisfied identically---made true by construction---once the fields are written in terms of potentials.

        Since $\nabla\cdot\vec{B} = 0$ everywhere, and since the divergence of any curl vanishes automatically, $\vec{B}$ may always be written as the curl of some vector field,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:vector-potential}
            \vec{B} = \nabla\times\vec{A},
        \end{equation}

        called the \emph{magnetic vector potential}. Substituting this into Faraday's law gives $\nabla\times\left(\vec{E} + \nicefrac{\partial\vec{A}}{\partial t}\right) = 0$, and a field whose curl vanishes is the gradient of something, so

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:scalar-potential}
            \vec{E} = -\nabla\varphi - \frac{\partial\vec{A}}{\partial t},
        \end{equation}

        where $\varphi$ is the \emph{electric scalar potential}. In electrostatics the second term is absent and $\varphi$ is the familiar potential whose difference between two points is a voltage.

        The gain is economy. The two source-free equations are now satisfied automatically and only the two with sources remain to be solved, and the six components of $\vec{E}$ and $\vec{B}$ are traded for four, $\varphi$ and $\vec{A}$. The potentials are not unique: adding $\nabla f$ to $\vec{A}$ while subtracting $\nicefrac{\partial f}{\partial t}$ from $\varphi$ leaves both fields exactly as they were, for any $f$. That freedom is called \emph{gauge invariance}, and it is used to impose whichever extra condition makes a given problem easiest. The potentials are also what enter the Lagrangian of a charged particle, and hence its quantum mechanics, so they are more than a calculational convenience.
