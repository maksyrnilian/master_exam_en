\section{Inertial frames, principles of relativity, the Galilean transformation, the Lorentz transformation and its consequences}
\label{sec:inertial-frames}

    An inertial frame is a frame of reference with respect to which every body, not subject to external interactions with other bodies, moves in uniform rectilinear motion or remains at rest. The existence of such a frame is postulated by Newton's first law of motion.

    The Galilean principle of relativity states that the laws of motion are identical in all inertial frames of reference, i.e.\ there is no privileged frame of reference. It was formulated in 1604 in the form of the following law: all frames of reference moving with respect to one another at constant velocity (including $\vec{v}=0$) are equivalent. Its consequence is the set of Galilean transformations between two frames of reference $(t,x,y,z)$ and $(t',x',y',z')$ with the primed one moving with velocity $v$ with respect to the unprimed one along the $x$ axis:

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:gallilean}
        \begin{aligned}
            t' &= t \\
            x' &= x - vt \\
            y' &= y \\
            z' &= z
        \end{aligned}
    \end{equation}

    Einstein additionally postulated that in every inertial frame of reference the speed of light has the same value. He made this assumption in 1905 based on an analysis of the transformations of Maxwell's equations, where he found that the electric and magnetic fields are not separately fixed: what one observer describes as a purely electric field, another observer moving with respect to the first describes as a mixture of an electric and a magnetic field. The two are aspects of one object rather than two independent fields.

    The Galilean transformations were thereby generalised to the Lorentz transformation to accommodate the Einstein's postulate:

    \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:lorentz}
        \begin{aligned}
            t' &= \gamma\left(t-xv/c^2\right) \\
            x' &= \gamma\left(x-tv\right) \\
            y' &= y \\
            z' &= z
        \end{aligned}
    \end{empheq}
    \keyeqentry{Lorentz transformation}

    where the parameter $\gamma$ is known as the Lorentz factor\footnote{In general, time dilation depends solely on the magnitude of the relative velocity between two frames, whereas length contraction is a directional phenomenon that occurs strictly parallel to the axis of motion---the $x$-axis in this standard configuration.}:

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:gamma}
        \gamma \equiv \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - \beta^2}} \geq 1, \qquad \beta \equiv \frac{v}{c} .
    \end{equation}

    Note that in the limit $\nicefrac{v}{c} \to 0$ we have $\gamma \to 1$ and the Lorentz transformation reduces to the Galilean one (\cref{eq:gallilean}).

    \needspace{4\baselineskip}
    The consequences of the Lorentz transformation include:
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item Time dilation, described by the formula $\Delta t = \gamma\Delta t_0$, where $\Delta t_0$ is the \emph{proper time}---time recorded in the rest frame of the timed event, and $\Delta t$ is the time recorded by another observer moving with velocity $v$ with respect to the first. An interval is \emph{shortest} in the frame in which it is at rest.
        \item Lorentz contraction, described by the formula $L = \nicefrac{L_0}{\gamma}$, where $L_0$ is the \emph{proper length}---length of an object at rest, and $L$ is the length of the object recorded by an observer moving with respect to it at velocity $v$. An object is \emph{longest} in the frame in which it is at rest.
        \item Relativity of simultaneity: two events determined by one observer to be simultaneous need not be simultaneous for another observer. Simultaneity translates to other frames only for the events that happen at the same point in space.
        \item The description of physical quantities in four dimensions. Since \cref{eq:lorentz} mixes $t$ with $x$, a split into a \enquote{time part} and a \enquote{space part} is not something all observers agree on. Related quantities are therefore collected into \emph{four-vectors}, whose four components transform into one another exactly as position does,
        \begin{equation*}
            x^{\mu} =
            \begin{bmatrix}
                ct \\ x \\ y \\ z
            \end{bmatrix} =
            \begin{bmatrix}
                ct \\ \vec{r}
            \end{bmatrix},
            \qquad
            p^{\mu} =
            \begin{bmatrix}
                \nicefrac{E}{c} \\ p_x \\ p_y \\ p_z
            \end{bmatrix} =
            \begin{bmatrix}
                \nicefrac{E}{c} \\ \vec{p}
            \end{bmatrix}.
        \end{equation*}
        
        Position is the prototype, and there the zeroth slot is literally time. What makes any other set of four quantities a four-vector is not an analogy with position but a transformation law. An equation written as an equality of four-vectors automatically holds in every inertial frame, because both sides transform in the same way. And each four-vector has one combination of its components that every observer agrees on. For position it is the \emph{spacetime interval} between two events, $\Delta s^{2} = c^{2}\Delta t^{2} - |\Delta\vec{r}|^{2}$, which measures whether the two are far enough apart in space that nothing could travel between them in the time available. If $\Delta s^{2} > 0$ the separation is \emph{timelike}, a body moving slower than light can be present at both events, one may therefore cause the other, and $\Delta s^{2} = c^{2}\Delta\tau^{2}$ gives the proper time a clock carried between them would record. If $\Delta s^{2} = 0$ the separation is \emph{lightlike} and only a light signal connects them. If $\Delta s^{2} < 0$ it is \emph{spacelike}, no signal can pass from one to the other, neither can influence the other, and there is an inertial frame in which the two are simultaneous---which is exactly the relativity of simultaneity above.
    \end{enumerate}

    \paragraph{Mass--energy}

        Writing out the invariant of the four-momentum gives the \emph{energy--momentum relation}

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:energy-momentum}
            E^{2} = p^{2}c^{2} + m^{2}c^{4},
        \end{empheq}
        \keyeqentry{Energy--momentum--mass relation}

        where $E$ is the total energy of a free body, $p$ the magnitude of its momentum, and $m$ its \emph{invariant mass}, meaning the mass measured in the frame in which the body is at rest. Two limits carry most of the physics. For a body at rest we have $p=0$ and the relation collapses to Einstein's $E=mc^{2}$, so that mass is itself a store of energy at the conversion rate $c^{2}\simeq\qty{9e16}{\joule\per\kilogram}$---the reason a milligram of mass defect suffices to power a nuclear reaction. For a massless particle such as the photon we have $m=0$ and the relation gives $E=pc$, a case $E=mc^{2}$ cannot describe at all. The invariant mass of a composite system also counts the internal energy of its parts, so a hot or compressed body is very slightly more massive than a cold relaxed one, and roughly \qty{99}{\percent} of a proton's mass is the kinetic energy of its quarks and the energy of the gluon field rather than the quark masses themselves.

\section{Fundamental interactions; carriers and ranges of interactions, charges and characteristic energy scales}
\label{sec:interactions}

    Every process in physics comes down to particles interacting, and every known interaction belongs to one of four families. In a quantum theory an interaction is not a force reaching across empty space but an \emph{exchange}: two particles interact by passing a third particle between them, called the \emph{interaction carrier}. The properties of the carrier---its mass, its spin, and the kind of charge it responds to---fix everything else, in particular how far the interaction reaches and how strong it is.

    \begin{figure}[htbp]
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.68\linewidth]{graphics/Standard_Model_of_Elementary_Particles.pdf}
        \caption{Standard Model of Elementary Particles.}
        \label{fig:standard-model-particles}
    \end{figure}

    What each interaction is responsible for:

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Strong.} Binds quarks into protons, neutrons and other \emph{hadrons}, meaning particles built out of quarks (\cref{sec:particles}). What is left over from this binding reaches slightly beyond each nucleon and holds protons and neutrons together in the nucleus. That residue is what overcomes the electrostatic repulsion between the protons and lets nuclei with more than one proton exist at all.
        \item \textbf{Electromagnetic.} Binds electrons to nuclei, atoms into molecules and molecules into solids, so all of chemistry and all of ordinary matter's rigidity. Light is a propagating disturbance of the same field.
        \item \textbf{Weak.} Converts one kind of quark or lepton into another. It drives beta decay, the first step of hydrogen fusion in stars, and essentially every interaction a neutrino ever has.
        \item \textbf{Gravitational.} Acts on everything that carries energy. It is by far the feeblest between two individual particles, but it dominates on astronomical scales because its charge---mass--energy---comes with one sign only and never cancels.
    \end{itemize}

    The first three are described by the \emph{Standard Model}\footnote{Energies in the table are quoted in \emph{electronvolts}, $1$~eV being the energy an electron gains across a potential difference of one volt, $1\,\mathrm{eV}=1.602\times10^{-19}$~J. Masses are quoted in the same units by \emph{mass--energy equivalence}, $E=mc^{2}$. Strictly the unit is then eV$/c^{2}$, but the $c^{2}$ is left out and one simply says the $W$ boson has a mass of $80$~GeV. In SI units $1$~eV$/c^{2}=1.783\times10^{-36}$~kg.} (\cref{fig:standard-model-particles}), the quantum field theory of elementary particles. Its matter content is twelve spin-\nicefrac{1}{2} particles arranged in three generations, six quarks and six leptons, classified in \cref{sec:particles}. \emph{Ordinary matter uses only the first generation (up and down quarks and the electron)} while the heavier generations are copies that decay quickly.

    The interactions are carried by spin-1 vector bosons: eight gluons, the photon, and the $W^{+}$, $W^{-}$ and $Z^{0}$ bosons. One further particle, the spin-0 scalar \emph{Higgs boson} of mass about $125$~GeV, is the quantum of the field that gives the $W$, the $Z$ and the charged fermions their masses. Gravitation stands outside this framework and has no accepted quantum description.

        \centering
        \renewcommand{\arraystretch}{1.25}
        \begin{tabular}{@{}llllc@{}}
            \hline
            \textbf{interaction} & \textbf{carrier} & \textbf{charge} & \textbf{range} & \textbf{strength} \\
            \hline

        \label{tab:interactions}
    \end{table}

    A particle feels an interaction only if it carries the matching charge, which is what the third column lists. \emph{Colour charge} comes in three values conventionally called \emph{red, green and blue,} and is carried by \emph{quarks and gluons} alone. \emph{Weak isospin} is the charge of the weak interaction and is carried by \emph{all fermions}. Its name records a mathematical analogy---it obeys the same algebra as spin---but it is not intrinsic angular momentum and has nothing to do with rotation in space. It sits in the charge column because that is the role it plays: it is the label deciding whether a particle couples to the interaction at all. 
    
    A neutrino has \emph{neither electric charge nor colour}, which is why it interacts \emph{only weakly} and passes through the Earth almost undisturbed. Carriers may themselves be charged: each gluon carries one colour and one anticolour, so gluons attract one another, whereas the photon is electrically neutral and ignores other photons. This self-attraction of the gluon field is the reason the strong interaction behaves so differently from the other three.

    \begin{wrapfigure}{L}{0.18\textwidth}
        \centering
        \includegraphics[width=0.16\textwidth]{graphics/Quark_structure_proton.pdf}
        \caption{Proton.}
        \label{fig:proton}
    \end{wrapfigure}\todo{reference in the appropriate secition}

    For example, the proton (\cref{fig:proton}) has quark content $uud$ and the neutron $udd$, and their electric charges follow by adding the quark charges (\cref{sec:particles}). Each \emph{baryon} must contain one quark of each colour, but the particular colour carried by each quark is not fixed: gluon exchange constantly reshuffles the assignment among the three, so only the combination as a whole is colour-neutral.

    The ranges in \cref{tab:interactions} are not independent facts but follow from the carrier masses. An exchanged carrier is never observed directly and so need not balance the energy books---it is \emph{virtual}, in effect borrowed from the vacuum provided the loan is repaid quickly. The energy--time uncertainty relation $\Delta E\,\Delta t\geq\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}$ (\cref{sec:uncertainty}) sets how quickly: borrowing at least the rest energy $mc^{2}$ limits its life to $\Delta t\sim\nicefrac{\hbar}{mc^{2}}$, and travelling at most at the speed of light it covers

    \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:yukawa-range}
        R \simeq c\,\Delta t \simeq \frac{h}{mc},
    \end{empheq}
    \keyeqentry{Compton range of an interaction}

    the Compton range of the carrier. A heavy carrier therefore gives a short-ranged interaction and a massless one an unlimited range, which is why electromagnetism and gravitation reach as far as they do.

    The strong interaction is the apparent exception, since its carrier is massless and yet it does not reach beyond the nucleus. Its range is set instead by \emph{confinement}: because gluons attract one another, colour charge can never be isolated, and gluons do not travel freely between well-separated particles. What acts between nucleons is a residual effect carried by \emph{pions}, the lightest hadrons, of rest energy about $140$~MeV, and \cref{eq:yukawa-range} then returns the familiar $1.4\times10^{-15}$~m.

    Beta decay shows the pieces working together. A down quark inside a neutron turns into an up quark and emits a $W^{-}$, which decays at once into an electron and an electron antineutrino, so that overall $n\to p+e^{-}+\bar{\nu}_{e}$. The energy released (kinetic energy shared among all resultant particles) is a few MeV, thousands of times less than the $80$~GeV needed to make a real $W$. The boson can therefore only ever be virtual, and it is this mismatch of scales---not any small coupling---that makes the weak interaction weak and slow at everyday energies.

    Collecting these, each interaction is characterised by three numbers that should be kept apart: the mass--energy of its carrier, the range that follows from it, and the energies at which the interaction shows up in practice.

    \begin{itemize}
        \item \textbf{Electromagnetic.} Carrier the photon, massless, so the range is unlimited. The interaction has no intrinsic energy scale of its own and the energies observed are set by the system: about $1$~eV for a chemical bond, $13.6$~eV to ionise hydrogen, tens of keV for the inner electrons of heavy atoms.
        \item \textbf{Strong.} The fundamental carriers, the eight gluons, are massless, so \cref{eq:yukawa-range} would give an unlimited range. Confinement removes it: colour cannot be separated, and what acts between nucleons is a residual interaction carried by pions of mass $140$~MeV, giving $R\simeq1.4\times10^{-15}$~m. Quark--quark binding runs to of order $1$~GeV and is what supplies almost all of a hadron's mass, whereas the residual nucleon--nucleon binding left over from it is only about $8$~MeV per nucleon.
        \item \textbf{Weak.} Carriers the $W^{\pm}$ at $80$~GeV and the $Z^{0}$ at $91$~GeV, giving $R\simeq2\times10^{-18}$~m. The carrier scale and the observed scale are far apart: the energy released in beta decay is only of order MeV, so the boson is always deeply virtual, and it is this mismatch, not a small coupling, that makes the interaction weak at everyday energies.
        \item \textbf{Gravitational.} The graviton, if it exists, is massless, so the range is unlimited and there is no carrier mass to set a scale. What gravitates is not rest mass but total mass--energy, so a particle accelerated to energy $E$ gravitates as though it had mass $\nicefrac{E}{c^{2}}$, and the gravitational attraction between two such particles grows as $E^{2}$ while the other interactions do not. The \emph{Planck energy} $E_{\mathrm{P}}=\sqrt{\hbar c^{5}/G}\approx10^{19}$~GeV is the point where that growth makes gravitation comparable to the rest, and hence where a quantum description would be needed. It is built from $\hbar$, $c$ and $G$ alone and lies beyond any conceivable accelerator.
    \end{itemize}

\section{Conservation laws in physics and their relations to symmetries}
\label{sec:conservation}

    The \emph{symmetries} of a physical system are closely connected with its \emph{conservation laws}. A symmetry here means that the system goes on behaving in exactly the same way after some transformation has been applied to it---shifting the whole experiment in time, moving it to another place, turning it to face another direction.

    Noether's theorem formalises the connection: to every continuous symmetry of the action there corresponds a conserved quantity. \emph{Continuous} means the transformation can be made arbitrarily small, as a rotation can but a mirror reflection cannot.

    \subsection{The Lagrangian, the action and the equation of motion}

        The \emph{Lagrangian} of a system is the difference between its kinetic and its potential energy,

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:lagrangian}
            \Lagr \equiv T-V,
        \end{empheq}
        \keyeqentry{Lagrangian}

        and the \emph{action} is the Lagrangian\footnote{Lagrangians are different for different paths. For example, for a freely moving particle $E=\nicefrac{1}{2}mv^2$ but $v(t)$ could take many forms which would differentiatie paths that start and end at the same points---a unifrom motion, a particle that accelerates and then decelerates etc.} accumulated along a path in time,

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:action}
            S \equiv \int_{t_1}^{t_2} \Lagr \, dt .
        \end{empheq}
        \keyeqentry{Action}

        $S$ is not a number belonging to the system but a number belonging to a whole candidate path, one value for every path one might consider. An object that eats a function and returns a number in this way is called a \emph{functional} (\cref{sec:variational}) as opposed to an ordinary function, which takes in a number and returns a number.

        Demanding that $S$ be \emph{stationary}---unchanged to first order when the path is deformed slightly with its two endpoints held fixed---singles out the path actually followed and yields the \emph{Euler--Lagrange equation},

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:euler-lagr}
            \frac{d}{dt} \left( \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{q}} \right) = \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial q},
        \end{empheq}
        \keyeqentry{Euler--Lagrange equation}

        which is a partiacl differential equation\footnote{
        A \emph{partial derivative} $\nicefrac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ is the rate of change of $f$ with respect to $x$ when every other argument of $f$ is held fixed, so its value depends entirely on which variables those are---thermodynamics makes this explicit by writing the held quantities as subscripts, as in $\left(\nicefrac{\partial U}{\partial V}\right)_{T}$. A \emph{total derivative} $\nicefrac{df}{dt}$ asks a different question: how $f$ changes along an actual path, counting every route by which $t$ influences it. The \emph{chain rule} connects the two,
        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}
            \frac{df}{dt} = \frac{\partial f}{\partial t} + \sum_{i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_{i}}\frac{dq_{i}}{dt},
        \end{empheq}
        for a function $f(q_{1},\dots,q_{n},t)$ whose arguments $q_{i}$ themselves vary with $t$. The first term is the \emph{explicit} dependence on $t$---what changes because the rule defining $f$ was written with a $t$ in it---and the sum is the \emph{implicit} dependence, what changes because the system has moved. This distinction is the whole content of several standard results.
        }
        where $q$ is any coordinate used to specify the configuration of the system and $\dot{q} = \nicefrac{dq}{dt}$. This is the principle of least action (\cref{sec:variational}) in one line, and for a single particle in a potential it returns Newton's second law (\cref{eq:newton2}).

    \subsection{Noether's theorem}

        \textit{If the action of a system is invariant under a continuous transformation, then there is a quantity built from the state of the system that does not change in time.}

        The theorem is stated for the action rather than for the equations of motion because the action is what those equations follow from, so a symmetry of $S$ is automatically a symmetry of the dynamics. In practice the symmetry is tested on $\Lagr$ instead, which is easier: $S$ is only $\Lagr$ integrated over time, so a transformation leaving $\Lagr$ unchanged leaves $S$ unchanged too.\footnote{The converse fails. $\Lagr$ may change by the total time derivative of some function and still leave $S$ stationary on the same paths, so invariance of $\Lagr$ is sufficient but not necessary.} Each example below therefore begins by asking which variable is missing from the Lagrangian.

    \subsection{Examples of the application of Noether's theorem}

        \subsubsection{Conservation of energy}

            Symmetry under a shift in time (homogeneity of time) leads to the law of conservation of energy. Suppose the Lagrangian does not depend explicitly on time, meaning that repeating the experiment tomorrow changes nothing,

            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial t} = 0 .
            \end{equation*}

            Differentiating $\Lagr$ along the trajectory and substituting \cref{eq:euler-lagr} then gives $\nicefrac{d}{dt}\left(\dot{q}\,\nicefrac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{q}} - \Lagr\right) = -\nicefrac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial t} = 0$, so that

            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{q}}\,\dot{q} - \Lagr = \const = \Ham,
            \end{equation*}

            which defines the \emph{Hamiltonian}. Whenever the kinetic energy is quadratic in the velocities and the coordinates carry no explicit time dependence---the ordinary case---this evaluates to

            \begin{equation}\label{eq:hamiltonian}
                \Ham \equiv T + V,
            \end{equation}

            the total energy of the system.

        \subsubsection{Conservation of momentum}

            Spatial symmetry (homogeneity of space) leads to the law of conservation of momentum. If the Lagrangian does not depend explicitly on position,

            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \vec{r}} = 0,
            \end{equation*}

            then inserting this into \cref{eq:euler-lagr} leads to

            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{d}{dt}\left( \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{\vec{r}}} \right) = 0.
            \end{equation*}

            With $ \vec{p} \equiv \nicefrac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{\vec{r}}} $, we have

            \begin{equation*}
                \frac{d\vec{p}}{dt} = 0 \, \Rightarrow \, \vec{p}=\const,
            \end{equation*}

            so that the momentum is conserved.

            For contrast, take a particle of mass $m$ in a uniform gravitational field, for which
            \begin{equation*}
                \Lagr = \tfrac{1}{2}m\dot{\vec{r}}^{\,2} - mgz .
            \end{equation*}
            This does depend on position, through $z$: moving the whole experiment upwards changes the potential energy, so space is not homogeneous in that direction. The vertical momentum is accordingly not conserved and the particle accelerates downwards. The horizontal coordinates $x$ and $y$ still do not appear, so $p_x$ and $p_y$ are conserved. A conservation law follows from a symmetry only in the directions in which that symmetry actually holds.

        \subsubsection{Conservation of angular momentum}

            Symmetry under rotation (isotropy of space) leads to the law of conservation of angular momentum. Turning the whole system through a small angle $\delta\vec{\phi}$ shifts every position by $\delta\vec{r} = \delta\vec{\phi} \times \vec{r}$ and every velocity by $\delta\dot{\vec{r}} = \delta\vec{\phi} \times \dot{\vec{r}}$. Requiring the Lagrangian not to notice,

            \begin{equation*}
                \delta\Lagr = \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \vec{r}} \cdot \delta\vec{r} + \frac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{\vec{r}}} \cdot \delta\dot{\vec{r}} = 0,
            \end{equation*}

            and substituting \cref{eq:euler-lagr} together with $\vec{p} \equiv \nicefrac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{\vec{r}}}$ collapses this to

            \begin{equation*}
                \delta\vec{\phi} \cdot \frac{d}{dt}\left( \vec{r} \times \vec{p} \right) = 0 .
            \end{equation*}

            The axis $\delta\vec{\phi}$ was arbitrary, so the bracket itself must be constant and the angular momentum is conserved,

            \begin{equation*}
                \vec{L} \equiv \vec{r} \times \vec{p} = \const .
            \end{equation*}

            In words: if turning the apparatus to face a different direction makes no difference to how it behaves, the combination $\vec{r} \times \vec{p}$ cannot change with time.\todoper{Watch Allan Adams on it.}

\section{Newton's laws of dynamics and the range of their applicability; classical mechanics as a limiting case of quantum mechanics; non-relativistic classical mechanics as a limiting case of relativistic classical mechanics}
\label{sec:newton}

    \subsection{Newton's laws of motion}

    Newton's laws of dynamics are three laws lying at the foundation of classical mechanics, specifying the relation between the motion of a body and the forces acting on it, which is why they are also called the laws of motion. In quantum mechanics they are for the most part inapplicable, and in relativistic mechanics they hold only within a limited range.

    \paragraph{Newton's first law}

        In an inertial frame of reference (\cref{sec:inertial-frames}), if no force acts on a body, or if the acting forces balance one another, then the body remains at rest or moves in uniform rectilinear motion.

        The first law assumes the existence of an inertial frame of reference but does not indicate how such a frame is to be sought, and it is therefore a postulate of the existence of an inertial frame of reference---\textit{there exists a frame of reference in which a particle, not subject to interaction with its surroundings, remains at rest or moves along a straight line with constant velocity.}

    \paragraph{Newton's second law}

        If an unbalanced force acts on a body, then the body moves with an acceleration directly proportional to the force and inversely proportional to the mass of the body.

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:newton2}
            \vec{F} = m \vec{a}.
        \end{equation}

    \paragraph{Newton's third law}

        Interactions between bodies are always mutual. In an inertial frame of reference the forces of mutual interaction of two bodies have the same magnitude, opposite directions, and different points of application (each acts on a different body).

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:newton3}
            \vec{F}_{12} = -\vec{F}_{21}
        \end{equation}

        In classical mechanics, Newton's third and second laws combined are equivalent with the law of conservation of momentum.

    \subsection{Limits of applicability of Newton's laws}

        Newton's laws of motion lose their direct validity in non-inertial frames, where it becomes necessary to introduce fictitious forces (sourceless, not subject to the 3rd law). Newtonian mechanics also becomes more complicated in more intricate coordinate systems\todo{intricate?}. In such cases the Lagrangian or Hamiltonian formalisms, based on the principle of least action (\cref{sec:variational}), work better.

        Quantum mechanics describes the behaviour of particles on a small scale, where Newtonian mechanics fails. The uncertainty principle (\cref{sec:uncertainty}), for example, forbids a particle a sharp position and a sharp momentum at the same time, whereas classical mechanics rests on \emph{exact trajectories}.

        The results of quantum mechanics in the limit of large quantum numbers should generally agree with the results of classical mechanics (Bohr's correspondence principle). In particular, \emph{assuming $\hbar \to 0$} may be regarded as a transition from a discrete to a continuous system. Some phenomena, however, have no classical limit at all: the stability of the atom and the discreteness of its spectrum (\cref{sec:quantisation}), tunnelling through a barrier (\cref{sec:tunnelling}), and the exclusion of identical fermions from a common state (\cref{eq:identical}), which is what gives matter its volume and the periodic table its structure. The atom is the sharpest failure. An electron on a circular orbit is accelerating, and classical electrodynamics says that an accelerating charge radiates, so the electron should lose energy continuously, emit a continuous spectrum rather than sharp lines, and spiral into the nucleus in roughly \qty{e-11}{\second}. Classical mechanics (electrodynamics) therefore do not merely mispredict the atom---\emph{they forbid ordinary matter from existing at all}.

        Moreover, Newton assumed that time is absolute, which was only countered 300 years later by Einstein and his theory of relativity, where the Galilean transformation is replaced by the Lorentz transformation (\cref{sec:inertial-frames}) and the two agree only to the extent that $\gamma \to 1$ for $v \ll c$ (\cref{eq:gamma}). For this reason classical mechanics still remains a useful tool for describing non-relativistic phenomena. Again, certain effects are inherently relativistic even though they are directly observable: cosmic-ray muons reach sea level in far greater numbers than their lifetime would allow without time dilation, and the atomic clocks aboard GPS satellites have to be corrected for both special- and general-relativistic effects or the fix drifts by kilometres a day.

\section{The postulates of quantum mechanics; measurement, state of a physical system}
\label{sec:postulates}

    Quantum mechanics rests on a handful of \emph{postulates}---statements that are not derived from anything deeper, but assumed, and then justified by the fact that everything they predict turns out to be correct.

    \subsection{The wave function}

        Classically, to know a particle is to know its position and momentum at every instant. Quantum mechanics replaces both by a single complex-valued function of position and time, the \emph{wave function} $\Psi(x,t)$.

        \begin{postulate}{the state}
            \label{post:state}
            The state of a system is completely described by a wave function $\Psi(x,t)$. Everything that can be known about the system is contained in it.
        \end{postulate}

        The wave function is not itself observable. Experiments return real numbers, and $\Psi$ is complex, so it cannot be measured. What can be measured is its squared modulus $|\Psi|^{2} = \Psi^{*}\Psi$, which is real and never negative.

        \begin{postulate}{the Born rule}
            \label{post:born}
            $|\Psi(x,t)|^{2}$ is a \emph{probability density}: the probability of finding the particle between $a$ and $b$ at time $t$ is
            \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:born-position}
                P_{a \le x \le b}(t) = \int^{b}_{a}|\Psi(x,t)|^{2}\,dx .
            \end{empheq}
            \keyeqentry{Born rule}
        \end{postulate}

        Because the particle must be somewhere, the wave function is \emph{normalised},

        \begin{equation*}
            \int^{\infty}_{-\infty}|\Psi(x,t)|^{2}\,dx = 1 .
        \end{equation*}

        This forces two requirements that do real work later. $\Psi$ must be \emph{single-valued}, meaning one value of $\Psi$ at each $(x,t)$, and it must die away fast enough at large $|x|$ for the integral to converge. Note also that multiplying $\Psi$ by a constant phase $e^{i\alpha}$ changes no probability, so two states differing only by such a \emph{global phase} are physically the same state.

    \subsection{Superposition}

        \begin{postulate}{superposition}
            \label{post:superposition}
            Any sum of possible states is itself a possible state: if $\Psi_{1}$ and $\Psi_{2}$ are states, so is $c_{1}\Psi_{1} + c_{2}\Psi_{2}$ with complex $c_{1}, c_{2}$.
        \end{postulate}\todo{c1 and c2 are normalised such that the particle is described by either of the wavefunctions which themsleves are normalised such that the total probability is 1.? so a single wavefunction is normalised differently if it is added to another to create a superposition?}

        If a system can be prepared in state A and in state B, it can be prepared in the combination \enquote{A + B}, realising both alternatives at once. The equation governing $\Psi$ turns out to be linear (\cref{eq:tdse}), so each term evolves on its own and the system stays a superposition at every later instant.

    \subsection{Why the wave function is complex: interference}

        A complex number whose squared modulus gives a probability is called a \emph{probability amplitude}. It carries a magnitude \emph{and} a phase, and the phase is what separates quantum from classical probability. Probabilities are positive and cannot cancel. Amplitudes can. This is forced on the theory rather than chosen for convenience: the $i$ sitting on the left of \cref{eq:tdse} means $\Psi$ cannot be a real function, and without a phase there would be nothing to cancel and no interference at all.

        Two rules govern them. Amplitudes for steps taken \emph{in succession} multiply. Amplitudes for \emph{alternative} routes to the same outcome add, provided nothing in the experiment records which route was taken. The squared modulus is taken only at the very end.

        In the double slit, let $A_{1}$ and $A_{2}$ be the amplitudes for a photon to reach a chosen point of the screen through slit 1 and slit 2. With both slits open and no record of which was used, the alternatives are \emph{indistinguishable}, the amplitudes add, and

        \begin{equation*}
            P = |A_{1} + A_{2}|^{2} = |A_{1}|^{2} + |A_{2}|^{2} + 2\,\mathrm{Re}\!\left(A_{1}^{*}A_{2}\right).
        \end{equation*}

        The last term is the \emph{interference term}. It depends on the relative phase of the two amplitudes, which shifts across the screen as the path difference changes, and it is what produces the fringes. Add a detector that records the slit and the alternatives become distinguishable, so it is now the probabilities that add, $P = |A_{1}|^{2} + |A_{2}|^{2}$, with no interference and no fringes. Amplitudes add when the alternatives are not distinguished, probabilities add when they are. The information that would tell them apart is called \emph{which-path information}, and merely having it available in the apparatus is enough to destroy the fringes.

    \subsection{Observables as operators}

        Position probabilities come straight out of \cref{eq:born-position}. Every other measurable quantity has to be extracted from $\Psi$, and the tool for that is an \emph{operator}: an instruction that takes one function and returns another. Position acts by multiplication and momentum by differentiation,

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:position-operator}
            \hat{x}\,\Psi = x\,\Psi, \qquad
            \hat{p} = -i\hbar\nabla, \qquad
            \hat{p}_{x} = -i\hbar\frac{\partial}{\partial x} .
        \end{empheq}
        \keyeqentry{Position operator}
        \keyeqentry{Momentum operator}

        The momentum operator is read off from the de Broglie relation $p = \hbar k$ (\cref{eq:de-broglie}): differentiating a plane wave $e^{ikx}$ brings down a factor $ik$, and $ik \cdot (-i\hbar) = \hbar k$. The operator of the total energy, the \emph{Hamiltonian}, is then built from it exactly as the classical $\Ham = T + V$ (\cref{eq:hamiltonian}) is,

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:hamiltonian-operator}
            \hat{H} = \frac{\hat{p}^{2}}{2m} + V(x) = -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\nabla^{2} + V(x) ,
        \end{empheq}
        \keyeqentry{Hamiltonian operator}

        where

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:nabla}
        \nabla \equiv
            \begin{bmatrix}
                \frac{\partial}{\partial x} \\[1ex] \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \\[1ex] \frac{\partial}{\partial z}
            \end{bmatrix}.
        \end{equation}

        \begin{postulate}{observables}
            \label{post:observables}
            To every measurable quantity $q$ there corresponds a \emph{Hermitian} operator $\hat{Q}$ acting on the wave function. Hermitian means $\hat{Q} = \hat{Q}^{\dag}$, where $\dag$ denotes transposition combined with complex conjugation.
        \end{postulate}

        The restriction is not arbitrary. Hermitian operators have real eigenvalues, as measured numbers must be, and their eigenfunctions belonging to different eigenvalues are orthogonal and together form a complete set. The possible outcomes are therefore mutually exclusive and exhaust the possibilities.

    \subsection{Eigenstates and measurement}

        An operator normally changes the function it acts on. The exceptional case is when it merely rescales it,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:eigenvalue}
            \hat{Q}\,\psi_{n} = q_{n}\,\psi_{n},
        \end{equation}

        where $\psi_{n}$ is an \emph{eigenfunction} of $\hat{Q}$ and the number $q_{n}$ its \emph{eigenvalue}. These are the states in which the observable has a definite value, and they are the exception rather than the rule: almost every state \emph{is not} an eigenfunction of a given operator.

        Because the eigenfunctions form a complete set, any state can be written as a sum of them,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:expansion}
            \Psi = \sum_{n}c_{n}\,\psi_{n}, \qquad c_{n} \in \mathbb{C},
        \end{equation}

        and because they are \emph{orthonormal}, $\int\psi_{n}^{*}\psi_{m}\,dx = \delta_{nm}$ (the Kronecker delta, $1$ when $n=m$ and $0$ otherwise), each coefficient is recovered by $c_{n} = \int\psi_{n}^{*}\Psi\,dx$.

        \begin{postulate}{measurement}
            \label{post:measurement}
            The only possible result of measuring $\hat{Q}$ is one of its eigenvalues $q_{n}$, obtained with probability $|c_{n}|^{2}$. Immediately after a measurement returning $q_{n}$, the system is in the corresponding eigenstate $\psi_{n}$.
        \end{postulate}

        The Born rule of \cref{eq:born-position} is the special case of this for position. The final sentence is the \emph{collapse} of the wave function, and it is the one step in the theory that is neither smooth nor reversible: a state carrying a whole range of possible answers becomes, abruptly, a single eigenfunction. Why this happens is unexplained and is called the \emph{measurement problem}. If several eigenfunctions share one eigenvalue (a \emph{degenerate} outcome), the measurement leaves behind only the part of the state built from those particular eigenfunctions, renormalised, rather than one definite eigenfunction.

        Which eigenfunctions you expand in depends on what you intend to measure. The eigenfunctions of $\hat{H}$ are not those of $\hat{p}$. The state is one object, written down in whichever set matches the apparatus, much like the same vector written in different bases.

        Applying \cref{eq:eigenvalue} to the energy gives the \emph{time-independent Schrödinger equation},

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:tise}
            \hat{H}\,\psi(x) = E\,\psi(x) .
        \end{empheq}
        \keyeqentry{Time-independent Schrödinger equation}

        Despite the name, this is not a wave equation---there is no time in it. It is an energy eigenvalue equation, and its solutions are the states of definite energy.

    \subsection{Where quantisation comes from}
    \label{sec:quantisation}

        Quantisation is \emph{not} a postulate. It falls out of \cref{eq:tise} once the conditions on $\Psi$ are imposed: the wave function must be finite, single-valued, normalisable and continuous, and its first derivative must be continuous as well wherever the potential is finite\footnote{Where the potential is infinite there is no tunnelling, the wave function is forced to zero at the boundary, and its derivative is allowed to jump there.}.

        When the particle is \emph{bound}, meaning trapped in a potential well, these conditions can be met only for particular values of $E$, and the energy spectrum is discrete. An unbound particle is not boxed in by them and its spectrum is continuous. The mechanism is the one that gives a guitar string fixed at both ends its discrete set of frequencies. The difference, and it is not a small one, is that here it is the \emph{energy} that is discrete, which classical physics never predicts.

        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Infinite square well} of width $L$: $E_{n} = \nicefrac{n^{2}\pi^{2}\hbar^{2}}{2mL^{2}}$. Tighter confinement means larger spacing. The lowest state has $E_{1} \neq 0$, the \emph{zero-point energy}: a confined quantum particle can never be completely at rest.
            \item \textbf{Harmonic potential} $V \propto x^{2}$: evenly spaced levels $E_{n} = \left(n + \nicefrac{1}{2}\right)\hbar\omega$, with gap $\hbar\omega$. Any potential minimum looks harmonic for small displacements, so this is the generic case for vibrations of molecules and solids.
            \item \textbf{Hydrogen atom}: the confining agent is the electrostatic attraction of the nucleus, $V(r) = -\nicefrac{e^{2}}{4\pi\varepsilon_{0}r}$, so the field of the nucleus is what does the confining. The bound states ($E<0$) form the discrete series $E_{n} = -\nicefrac{13.6\,\mathrm{eV}}{n^{2}}$, crowding together as $n$ grows because the well is shallower further out. Above $E = 0$ the electron is free and the spectrum is continuous.
            \item \textbf{Angular momentum} is quantised for a related but distinct reason: the wave function must return to itself after a full turn, which forces $L_{z} = m\hbar$ with integer $m$. This is confinement in angle rather than in radius.
            \item \textbf{Free particle}: confined by nothing, so neither its energy nor its momentum is quantised.
        \end{itemize}

        Light is also exchanged in quanta, $E = hf = \hbar\omega$. This does \emph{not} follow from the postulates above, which describe a fixed set of particles and say nothing about the electromagnetic field. It is an experimental input, and it is recovered as a result only once the field itself is treated quantum-mechanically.

    \subsection{Time evolution}

        \begin{postulate}{time evolution}
            \label{post:evolution}
            Between measurements the state evolves according to the time-dependent Schrödinger equation,
            \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:tdse}
                i\hbar\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t} = \hat{H}\Psi .
            \end{empheq}
            \keyeqentry{Time-dependent Schrödinger equation}
        \end{postulate}

        The Schrödinger equation is a guess. It is not derived from anything deeper, and the reasoning behind it runs backwards, from the answer to the equation. Start from a de Broglie wave, $\Psi \propto e^{i(kx-\omega t)}$, and from two relations already supplied by experiment, $E = \hbar\omega$ and $p = \hbar k$. What we want to reproduce is the ordinary classical statement $E = \nicefrac{p^{2}}{2m} + V$, so look at the powers in it: energy appears once, momentum appears squared. We therefore need something that pulls \emph{one} factor of $\omega$ out of the wave, and something that pulls \emph{two} factors of $k$ out of it.

        Differentiation does precisely that, because each derivative of an exponential brings its exponent down as a multiplying factor. Dressed with the right constants,

        \begin{align*}
            i\hbar\frac{\partial \Psi}{\partial t} &= i\hbar\left(-i\omega\right)\Psi = \hbar\omega\,\Psi = E\,\Psi , \\[4pt]
            -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\frac{\partial^{2} \Psi}{\partial x^{2}} &= -\frac{\hbar^{2}}{2m}\left(ik\right)^{2}\Psi = \frac{\hbar^{2}k^{2}}{2m}\,\Psi = \frac{p^{2}}{2m}\,\Psi .
        \end{align*}

        This is the point of the exercise. Each combination hands back \emph{the same wave multiplied by the number we were after}, which is exactly the eigenvalue pattern of \cref{eq:eigenvalue}: differentiation is the machinery that extracts a physical number from a wave. Substituting these two expressions for $E$ and $\nicefrac{p^{2}}{2m}$ in the classical energy statement gives \cref{eq:tdse}. The equation is, in effect, reverse-engineered---the simplest linear equation whose plane-wave solutions carry the right energy and the right momentum. It is not a fundamental law in the way Maxwell's equations are. It is kept because everything it predicts turns out to be right.

        Because the equation is \emph{first} order in time, unlike a classical wave equation, the state at one instant fixes it at every other. The evolution keeps the total probability equal to $1$ and is fully reversible, and evolution of that kind is called \emph{unitary}. It stands in sharp contrast to the abrupt, irreversible collapse of the measurement postulate, and the tension between the two \emph{is} the measurement problem.

        If the Hamiltonian does not itself depend on time, \cref{eq:tdse} separates into a space part and a time part. The space part is \cref{eq:tise}, and the full solution differs from it only by a rotating phase,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:stationary}
            \Psi(x,t) = \psi(x)\,e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}Et} .
        \end{equation}

        This fixes the convention used throughout the note: capital $\Psi$ or $\Phi$ is the full, time-dependent wave function, lower-case $\psi$ or $\phi$ the time-independent part alone. Such a state is called \emph{stationary}, because the phase cancels in every squared modulus and so no probability changes with time. The phase turns at angular frequency $\omega = \nicefrac{E}{\hbar}$, so $E = \hbar\omega$ appears here as a property of the state, not as a statement about photons.

        On its own a rotating phase does nothing. In a superposition it does a great deal. Take a state built from two stationary states of different energy,

        \begin{equation*}
            \Psi = c_{1}\psi_{1}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}E_{1}t} + c_{2}\psi_{2}e^{-\frac{i}{\hbar}E_{2}t} .
        \end{equation*}

        The two phases turn at different rates, so the phase \emph{difference} between the terms grows steadily, and the interference term in $|\Psi|^{2}$ oscillates at the frequency $\omega_{21} = \nicefrac{\left(E_{2}-E_{1}\right)}{\hbar}$. The probability density sloshes back and forth at that frequency instead of standing still, and for an electron in an atom $\omega_{21}$ is precisely the frequency of the light emitted or absorbed on that transition. A \emph{global} phase changes nothing, a \emph{relative} phase changes everything.\todo{that related to fermi golden rule?}

    \subsection{States as vectors: Dirac notation}

        \Cref{eq:expansion} fixes the state entirely by the list of coefficients $c_{n}$, and a list of numbers is a vector. This is the starting point of Heisenberg's \emph{matrix mechanics}: wave functions are vectors, and the eigenfunctions are the basis vectors of the space they live in.

        That space is a \emph{Hilbert space} $\Hilb$: a complex vector space equipped with a scalar product, of finite or infinite dimension. The abstract vector is written $\ket{\Psi}$ and called a \emph{ket}, so that \cref{eq:expansion} becomes $\ket{\Psi} = \sum_{n}c_{n}\ket{\psi_{n}}$. In a basis, $\ket{\Psi}$ is a column of complex numbers, and the \emph{bra} $\bra{\Psi} = \ket{\Psi}^{\dag}$ is the corresponding row with every entry conjugated.\footnote{The two possible orders of multiplication give quite different objects. Row times column, $\braket{\Phi|\Psi} = \begin{bmatrix} b_{1}^{*} & b_{2}^{*} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} c_{1} \\ c_{2} \end{bmatrix} = b_{1}^{*}c_{1} + b_{2}^{*}c_{2}$, is $1\times2$ times $2\times1$, so the result is $1\times1$: a single number. Column times row, $\ket{\Psi}\bra{\Phi} = \begin{bmatrix} c_{1} \\ c_{2} \end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix} b_{1}^{*} & b_{2}^{*} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} c_{1}b_{1}^{*} & c_{1}b_{2}^{*} \\ c_{2}b_{1}^{*} & c_{2}b_{2}^{*} \end{bmatrix}$, is $2\times1$ times $1\times2$, so the result is $2\times2$: a matrix, that is, an operator.}

        The scalar product is inherited from the overlap integral of two wave functions,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:inner-product}
            \braket{\Phi|\Psi} = \int \Phi^{*}(x)\,\Psi(x)\,dx = \sum_{n}b_{n}^{*}c_{n} \in \mathbb{C},
        \end{equation}

        where $b_{n}$ and $c_{n}$ are the expansion coefficients of the two states. The conjugation makes it Hermitian rather than symmetric, $\braket{\Phi|\Psi} = \braket{\Psi|\Phi}^{*}$, so that $\braket{\Psi|\Psi} \ge 0$ is real and its square root is the length of the vector. Orthonormality reads $\braket{\psi_{n}|\psi_{m}} = \delta_{nm}$ with the Kronecker delta as before, the coefficients are recovered as $c_{n} = \braket{\psi_{n}|\Psi}$, and the measurement postulate takes its compact form,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:born-amplitude}
            P_{n} = \left|\braket{\psi_{n}|\Psi}\right|^{2}.
        \end{equation}

        In this language an operator on an $m$-dimensional space is an $m \times m$ matrix, Hermiticity is the statement that the matrix equals its own conjugate transpose, and \cref{eq:eigenvalue} is an ordinary matrix eigenvalue problem.

    \subsection{The simplest example: spin}

        Some degrees of freedom have no wave function in position at all, and for these the vector language is the only one available. Electron spin is the smallest case, a two-dimensional Hilbert space (spin itself is treated in \cref{sec:angular-momentum}). Take the states $\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow}$, spin up and down along $z$, as the basis:

        \begin{equation*}
            \ket{\uparrow} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
            \ket{\downarrow} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}, \qquad
            \hat{S}_{z} = \frac{\hbar}{2}\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{bmatrix}.
        \end{equation*}

        Multiplying out gives $\hat{S}_{z}\ket{\uparrow} = +\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\hat{S}_{z}\ket{\downarrow} = -\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}\ket{\downarrow}$, so the basis states are eigenvectors and the only possible results are $\pm\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}$. Now take the superposition

        \begin{equation*}
            \ket{\Psi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\uparrow} + \ket{\downarrow}\right)
            = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 1 \end{bmatrix}.
        \end{equation*}

        The amplitude for spin up is $\braket{\uparrow|\Psi} = \nicefrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}$, so by \cref{eq:born-amplitude} the probability is $\nicefrac{1}{2}$, and likewise for spin down. Acting with $\hat{S}_{z}$ gives $\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}\cdot\nicefrac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\uparrow}-\ket{\downarrow}\right)$, which is not a multiple of $\ket{\Psi}$: the operator has genuinely rotated the vector, as it does for any state that is not one of its eigenvectors. The same $\ket{\Psi}$ is nonetheless an exact eigenvector of $\hat{S}_{x} = \nicefrac{\hbar}{2}\left[\begin{smallmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{smallmatrix}\right]$ with eigenvalue $+\nicefrac{\hbar}{2}$. Having a sharp $x$-component costs it all knowledge of the $z$-component, which is the uncertainty principle (\cref{sec:uncertainty}) in its simplest form.

    \subsection{Mean values}

        Ordinary probability theory defines a mean in two equivalent ways. For a quantity with discrete possible values $q_{n}$ occurring with probabilities $P_{n}$, the mean is $\sum_{n}q_{n}P_{n}$. For a continuous variable with normalised probability density $P(x)$, it is $\ev{x} = \int xP(x)\,dx$.

        Quantum mechanics uses exactly these, with $P_{n} = |c_{n}|^{2}$. A single measurement returns one eigenvalue. Repeating it on many identically prepared systems gives a distribution whose mean is

        \begin{equation*}
            \ev{\hat{Q}} = \sum_{n}q_{n}|c_{n}|^{2} = \bra{\Psi}\hat{Q}\ket{\Psi} = \int \Psi^{*}(x)\,\hat{Q}\,\Psi(x)\,dx .
        \end{equation*}

        The forms agree because the operator can be rebuilt from its own outcomes and eigenstates,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:spectral}
            \hat{Q} = \sum_{n}q_{n}\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}},
        \end{equation}

        where each $\ket{\psi_{n}}\bra{\psi_{n}}$ is a \emph{projection operator}, a matrix that discards everything in a state except its $\ket{\psi_{n}}$ component. (\Cref{eq:spectral} as written assumes a discrete, non-degenerate spectrum.) The mean is generally \emph{not} one of the possible outcomes: for the spin state above $\ev{\hat{S}_{z}} = 0$, a value never seen in any single run.

    \subsection{Systems with several degrees of freedom}

        If a system has more than one degree of freedom (a photon carries both a position and a polarisation), each degree of freedom has its own Hilbert space $\Hilb_{i}$.

        \begin{postulate}{composite systems}
            \label{post:composite}
            The Hilbert space of a composite system is the \emph{tensor product} of the spaces of its parts, $\Hilb = \bigotimes_{i}\Hilb_{i}$.\footnote{The tensor product $\Hilb_{1} \otimes \Hilb_{2}$ is the space whose basis vectors are all the pairs formed by taking one basis vector from $\Hilb_{1}$ and one from $\Hilb_{2}$. Its dimensions therefore multiply rather than add: a two-state spin combined with a three-state degree of freedom gives a six-dimensional space, not a five-dimensional one.}
        \end{postulate}

        A state that cannot be written as a single such pair is called \emph{entangled}. Take two spins in\todoper{look at quantum erases experiment}

        \begin{equation*}
            \ket{\Psi} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\left(\ket{\uparrow\uparrow} + \ket{\downarrow\downarrow}\right),
        \end{equation*}

        where $\ket{\uparrow\downarrow}$ means the first spin up and the second down. No choice of a state for the first spin and a state for the second reproduces this, so neither particle has a state of its own---only the pair does.

        It follows from what is already above rather than from anything new. By the superposition postulate the pair may sit in a combination of the two joint alternatives, and by the measurement postulate the collapse applies to the pair as a whole. Measuring the first spin gives up or down with probability $\nicefrac{1}{2}$ each, and whichever comes out, the state collapses to a single term and the second spin is then certain to match, however far apart the two have been carried. This is why the tensor product is more than bookkeeping: most states in the product space are not products at all.

    \subsection{Summary}

        There are seven postulates of quantum mechanics.

        \begin{enumerate}
            \item \textbf{State}---the state of a system is completely described by its wave function, and everything that can be known about the system is contained in it.
            \item \textbf{Born rule}---the squared modulus of the wave function is a probability density.
            \item \textbf{Superposition}---any sum of possible states is itself a possible state.
            \item \textbf{Observables}---every measurable quantity corresponds to a Hermitian operator acting on the wave function.
            \item \textbf{Measurement}---the only possible result is one of that operator's eigenvalues, obtained with a probability given by the squared modulus of the corresponding expansion coefficient, and immediately afterwards the state is the matching eigenfunction.
            \item \textbf{Time evolution}---between measurements the state evolves smoothly and reversibly under the time-dependent Schrödinger equation.
            \item \textbf{Composite systems}---the Hilbert space of a composite system is the tensor product of the spaces of its parts.
        \end{enumerate}

\section{Angular momentum and precession --- classical and quantum description}
\label{sec:angular-momentum}

    The classical angular momentum of a system is defined as

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:momentum}
        \vec{L} \equiv \sum_i \vec{r}_i \times \vec{p}_i,
    \end{equation}

    where $\vec{r}_i$ is the position and $\vec{p}_i$ the momentum of the $i$-th element of the system. It is the quantity conserved when space is isotropic (\cref{sec:conservation}).

    In quantum mechanics the same expression is promoted to an operator (\cref{post:observables}), the \emph{orbital} angular momentum

    \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:orbital-angular-momentum}
        \hat{\vec{L}} = -i\hbar\left(\vec{r}\times \nabla\right).
    \end{empheq}
    \keyeqentry{Orbital angular momentum operator}

    A particle also carries \emph{spin}, an angular momentum that is not associated with any motion through space. It transforms under rotations exactly as $\hat{\vec{L}}$ does, but it has no classical counterpart and is not a picture of the particle turning about itself. Like $\hat{\vec{L}}$ it is a vector of three operators, one observable per axis,

    \begin{equation}\label{eq:spin-angular-momentum}
        \hat{\vec{S}} \equiv
            \begin{bmatrix}
                \hat{S}_{x} \\ \hat{S}_{y} \\ \hat{S}_{z}
            \end{bmatrix},
    \end{equation}

    and for an electron each component is a $2\times2$ matrix. This is the operator already used as the worked example in \cref{sec:postulates}, where $\hat{S}_{z}$ was written out, its eigenvectors $\ket{\uparrow}$ and $\ket{\downarrow}$ identified, and a measurement on a superposition of them followed through. That discussion is the concrete content of \cref{eq:spin-angular-momentum} and is not repeated here.

    The total angular momentum is the sum of the two,

    \begin{equation*}
        \hat{\vec{J}} = \hat{\vec{L}} + \hat{\vec{S}} .
    \end{equation*}

    \subsection{Quantisation and quantum numbers}

        Only two things about an angular momentum can be known at once: the magnitude of its square, and one chosen component, conventionally the one along $z$. The other two components then have no definite value. Both of the knowable quantities are always quantised---it comes not from confinement but from the restrictions on the wave function (symmetry under rotation, normalisability etc. (\cref{sec:quantisation})),

        \begin{equation*}
            \hat{L}^{2}\ket{l,m} = \hbar^{2}l(l+1)\ket{l,m}, \qquad
            \hat{L}_{z}\ket{l,m} = \hbar m\ket{l,m},
        \end{equation*}

        with $l = 0,1,2,\dots$ and $m = -l,-l+1,\dots,+l$, so an angular momentum of a given magnitude has $2l+1$ allowed orientations. The same pattern holds for $\hat{\vec{S}}$ with the pair $(s,m_s)$ and for $\hat{\vec{J}}$ with $(j,m_j)$, except that $s$ and $j$ are allowed to be half-integer. An electron always has $s = \nicefrac{1}{2}$ and therefore $m_s = \pm\nicefrac{1}{2}$, which is where its two states come from. Spin is unconditional in this way, but orbital angular momentum is not: $\hat{\vec{L}}$ contains $\vec{r}$ and is therefore defined only relative to a chosen origin, so $l$ is a meaningful label only when something picks that origin out, as the nucleus does in an atom. Even then it may be zero, as it is for an electron in an $s$ orbital.

        Since $\hat{\vec{L}}$ and $\hat{\vec{S}}$ belong to the same electron, they combine into $\hat{\vec{J}}$, and $j$ then runs from $|l-s|$ to $l+s$ in integer steps---for $l=1$ and $s=\nicefrac{1}{2}$, either $j=\nicefrac{1}{2}$ or $j=\nicefrac{3}{2}$. The two combinations differ slightly in energy because the electron's magnetic moment sits in the magnetic field produced by its own orbital motion, and that splitting is the \emph{fine structure} of the spectrum. How the quantum numbers $n$, $l$, $m$ and $m_{s}$ label the states of an atom, and how large the fine and hyperfine splittings are beside the gross structure, is set out in \cref{sec:atom-molecule}.

    \subsection{Classical precession}

        Precession is the slow circular drift of the axis of a spinning object. Instead of pointing in a fixed direction, the spin axis itself sweeps around, tracing out the surface of a cone. The familiar example is a spinning toy top: as it spins, its axis does not simply fall over under gravity but slowly circles around the vertical.

        The angular momentum $\vec{L}$ of the spinning body points along its spin axis, and a torque changes it at the rate $\nicefrac{d\vec{L}}{dt} = \vec{\tau}$. If the torque is perpendicular to $\vec{L}$, then so is the increment it adds, so it turns $\vec{L}$ without lengthening it and the axis sweeps out a cone. For the toy top, gravity acting at the centre of mass supplies the torque, and unless the axis stands exactly vertical that torque is horizontal and therefore perpendicular to $\vec{L}$. The axis moves not along the force but along the torque, which is what makes precession look counter-intuitive.\todo{SIGNIFICANT EDIT (deferred): document-wide notation sweep---separate notation should be used for quantum and classical angular momentum, and for spin and orbital angular momentum in the entire document. all operators should carry hats}

        The precession frequency is given by

        \begin{equation*}
            \omega_p = \frac{\tau}{I_s\omega_s\sin(\theta)}
        \end{equation*}

        where $\tau$ is the torque, $I_s$ the moment of inertia, $\omega_s$ the spin angular velocity, and $\theta$ the angle between the spin axis and the vertical axis about which it precesses.

    \subsection{Quantum precession}
    \label{subsec:quantum-precession}

        A particle such as an electron carries a magnetic moment $\vec{\mu}$ associated with its spin angular momentum\footnote{
        An orbital moment behaves the same way, $\vec{\mu} = \gamma\vec{L}$, but with a gyromagnetic ratio half as large. In an atom with unpaired electrons the spin contribution is usually the dominant one, whereas in a diamagnet the spins are paired and only the orbital part survives (\cref{sec:diamagnets}).
        }$\vec{S}$---the two are proportional, $\vec{\mu} = \gamma\vec{S}$ with $\gamma$ the \emph{gyromagnetic ratio}, and so lie along the same axis. Placed in an external magnetic field $\vec{B}$, the moment feels a torque

        \begin{equation*}
            \vec{\tau} = \vec{\mu}\times \vec{B},
        \end{equation*}

        which is perpendicular to $\vec{S}$ and therefore, exactly as in the classical case, makes $\vec{S}$ sweep around the direction of $\vec{B}$ instead of aligning with it. This is \emph{Larmor precession}, the direct analogue of gravity making a toy top precess, with the spin angular momentum $\vec{S}$ playing the role of the top's angular momentum $\vec{L}$.

        The rate of this precession, the \emph{Larmor frequency} $\omega_{L} = \gamma B$, depends only on the field strength and not at all on how far $\vec{S}$ is tilted away from $\vec{B}$, because the two factors of $\sin\theta$ that enter the torque and the cone radius cancel. Every spin in a sample therefore turns at the same rate whatever its orientation, which is what makes the effect usable for magnetic resonance.
        
        The same torque acts on an orbital moment, so an electron's orbit precesses too---the electron circulates as before, while the plane of its orbit is carried slowly around the field axis. The difference is that swinging an orbit round in this way carries charge with it, which a swinging spin does not, so the precession is itself a small current whose moment opposes the applied field. That is the origin of \emph{Langevin diamagnetism} (\cref{sec:diamagnets}), and since every orbit precesses at the same rate their moments simply add.

        This is \emph{Larmor precession}: the precession of any magnetic moment about an applied field, whether that moment comes from spin or from orbital motion. It is the direct analogue of gravity making a toy top precess, with the spin angular momentum $\vec{S}$ playing the role of the top's own angular momentum.

\section{Variational principles in physics; examples of equations that can be derived from variational principles}
\label{sec:variational}\todoper{google other principles}

    \subsection{What a variational principle is}\label{sec:variational-intro}

        A \emph{variational principle} says that the shape or motion a system actually adopts is the one that makes some scalar quantity stationary among all the alternatives one could imagine. That quantity is a \emph{functional}---a rule that swallows a whole function, such as a curve or a trajectory, and returns a single number. \emph{Stationary} means that any small deformation of that function, with the endpoints held fixed, leaves the number unchanged to first order, written $\delta S = 0$ for a functional $S$ \footnote{
            It is the same condition as $f'(x) = 0$ at a turning point of an ordinary function, with the single variable $x$ replaced by an entire function. Only in the simplest cases is the answer a true minimum---a maximum or a saddle satisfies the condition equally well---so the traditional word \enquote{least} is something of a historical misnomer.}.
        The machinery for turning $\delta S = 0$ into a differential equation is the \emph{calculus of variations} of Euler and Lagrange, built for exactly this class of problem.

        This is the opposite of Newton's way of describing motion. Newton's is \emph{local}: at each instant the force fixes the acceleration, the acceleration updates the velocity, and the trajectory is assembled step by step. It requires every force to be known as a vector and resolved along fixed axes, forces of constraint included. A variational principle takes a \emph{global} view, comparing whole candidate histories at once, and it needs only scalars---typically $T$ and $V$---in whatever coordinates suit the problem.

        Variational principles come in two broad families. In \emph{statics} the functional is usually an energy, and equilibrium is the configuration that minimises it. A chain hanging from two posts settles into the curve that puts its centre of mass as low as possible, subject to the chain having a fixed length. The answer is the \emph{catenary} $y = a\cosh\!\left(\nicefrac{x}{a}\right)$, found in 1691 by Huygens, Leibniz and Johann Bernoulli after Galileo had guessed a parabola. A soap film spanning a wire loop likewise takes the shape of least area, because its energy is proportional to its surface. The fixed-length requirement is a \emph{constraint}: a condition the candidate curves must satisfy, imposed by adding it to the functional with a Lagrange multiplier.

        In \emph{dynamics} the functional is the \emph{action} $S$ (\cref{eq:action}), an integral accumulated along a candidate path in time, which can be evaluated for any such path and not only for the true one. Requiring $\delta S = 0$ between fixed endpoints is the \emph{principle of least action}, and it yields the Euler--Lagrange equations of motion.

        The idea began in optics as \emph{Fermat's principle}: light travels between two points along the route taking the least time, from which the laws of reflection and refraction follow (\cref{eq:snell}). The standard textbook problem is the \emph{brachistochrone} of Johann Bernoulli (1696): of all ramps joining a point to a lower one, find the shape down which a bead released from rest slides in the shortest time. The answer is a cycloid, the curve traced by a point on the rim of a rolling wheel.

    \subsection{Equations that follow from it}

        \begin{itemize}
            \item \textbf{Euler--Lagrange equation.} Applying $\delta S = 0$ to the mechanical action, whose integrand is the Lagrangian (\cref{eq:lagrangian}), turns \enquote{choose the best path} into a differential equation the path must satisfy at every instant (\cref{eq:euler-lagr}, derived in \cref{sec:conservation}). For a single particle it returns Newton's second law (\cref{eq:newton2}), but it holds in any \emph{generalised coordinates} $q_i$---any set of numbers that fixes the configuration, such as an angle or a distance along a track---which is what makes it far easier than Newton's method for constrained or awkwardly shaped systems.
            \item \textbf{Noether's theorem.} Because the equations of motion follow from the action, every continuous symmetry of the action forces a quantity to be conserved: time-translation gives energy, spatial translation momentum, rotation angular momentum (\cref{sec:conservation}). Conservation laws are thus a consequence of the variational formulation, not separate assumptions.
            \item \textbf{Hamilton's equations.} Trading each velocity $\dot{q}_i$ for the corresponding momentum $p_i = \nicefrac{\partial \Lagr}{\partial \dot{q}_i}$---a \emph{Legendre transformation}, the standard trick of relabelling a function by its own slope---replaces one second-order equation per coordinate by two first-order ones, $\dot{q}_i = \nicefrac{\partial \Ham}{\partial p_i}$ and $\dot{p}_i = -\nicefrac{\partial \Ham}{\partial q_i}$, governed by the Hamiltonian (\cref{eq:hamiltonian}). The content is unchanged, but this is the form statistical physics and quantum mechanics are built on.
            \item \textbf{Hamilton--Jacobi equation.} Here the action itself becomes the unknown. Regarded as a function of the point where the path ends, $S$ obeys the single equation $\Ham\!\left(q_i,\nicefrac{\partial S}{\partial q_i},t\right) + \nicefrac{\partial S}{\partial t} = 0$, every momentum having been replaced by a derivative of $S$. Solving it is an alternative route to the motion, and its form is strikingly close to the Schrödinger equation (\cref{eq:tdse}), which is why it is regarded as the classical doorway to wave mechanics.
            \item \textbf{Feynman's path integral.} The quantum version of the whole scheme. Instead of selecting one path it gives every path a contribution $e^{iS/\hbar}$ and adds them all. Where $S$ is large compared with $\hbar$, neighbouring paths arrive with wildly different phases and cancel, except near the stationary path where the phase barely varies---so the classical principle is recovered as a limit rather than assumed.
            \item \textbf{Maxwell's equations.} The same rule applied to a field rather than a particle: the candidate is now a field configuration filling space and time, and the action of the electromagnetic field together with its coupling to charges is stationary exactly for those configurations obeying Maxwell's equations (\cref{eq:maxwell-general}).
            \item \textbf{Einstein's field equations.} The Einstein--Hilbert action, whose integrand measures the curvature of spacetime, yields the field equations of general relativity. That one rule generates the governing equations of mechanics, electromagnetism and gravitation alike is what makes the variational viewpoint one of the unifying ideas of physics.
            \item \textbf{Classic individual problems.} Many standard problems are variational rather than dynamical: a functional is written down and made stationary, often subject to a constraint. The laws of reflection and refraction follow from Fermat's principle of least time (\cref{sec:em-waves,eq:snell}), the brachistochrone and the hanging chain from least travel time and least potential energy respectively (\cref{sec:variational-intro}), the soap film from least area and others.
        \end{itemize}

\section{The laws of thermodynamics}
\label{sec:thermodynamics}

    \emph{Thermal equilibrium} implies there is no net flow of thermal energy between two systems that can exchange heat. A system is said to be in thermal equilibrium with itself if the temperature within the system is spatially uniform and temporally constant.

    \subsection{The zeroth law of thermodynamics}

        If systems A and B, capable of exchanging heat with one another, are in thermal equilibrium with each other, and the same is true of systems B and C, then systems A and C are also in thermal equilibrium with each other.

    \subsection{The first law of thermodynamics}

        The change in the internal energy of a system is equal to the sum of the heat supplied and the \emph{work done on the system}.

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:first-law}
            \Delta U = Q + W,
        \end{equation}

        or in a differential form:

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:first-law-differential}
            dU = \delta Q - p\, dV,
        \end{equation}

        where $dV$ is the change in volume of the system (positive when the system expands---it does work and loses internal energy, $W<0$).

    \subsection{The second law of thermodynamics}

        The second law has many different phrasings, but they all say roughly the same thing. They prohibit certain processes from happening. 

        \begin{itemize}
            \item Clausius statement: \textit{There is no thermodynamic process whose sole result would be the absorption of heat from a reservoir at a lower temperature and its transfer to a reservoir at a higher temperature.}
            \item Kelvin statement: \textit{No process is possible whose sole effect would be the absorption of a certain quantity of heat from a reservoir and its conversion into an equivalent amount of work.}
            \item Planck statement: \textit{It is impossible to construct an engine which will work in a complete cycle, and produce no effect except the production of work and cooling of a heat reservoir.}
        \end{itemize}

        These statements are made quantitative by the \emph{Clausius inequality}, which holds for any process:

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:clausius-inequality}
            dS \geq \frac{\delta Q}{T}.
        \end{equation}

        Here $S$ is the \emph{entropy} and $T$ the temperature at which the heat $\delta Q$ crosses the boundary. Entropy\todo{maybe move to thermodynamics section or reference it here} is a \emph{function of state}, meaning its change between two equilibrium states depends only on those two states and not on the path taken between them, and that is what makes the left-hand side usable even though no instrument reads entropy directly.
        
        To obtain $\Delta S$ for a real process, however violent, one connects the same two end states by \emph{any} convenient reversible path and integrates $\nicefrac{\delta Q}{T}$ along that one instead. \Cref{eq:clausius-inequality} then measures the real process against its reversible ideal: the entropy actually gained is never less than the heat transfer accounts for, and the excess is entropy generated by the irreversibility itself. This is what caps the efficiency of every heat engine and fixes which way a process is allowed to run. The equality holds only for reversible processes. For an isolated system ($\delta Q = 0$) it reduces to

        \begin{equation*}
            dS \geq 0,
        \end{equation*}

        so \emph{spontaneous processes drive the system towards higher entropy and equilibrium is the state of maximum entropy}. This macroscopic, thermodynamic entropy coincides with the microscopic count of accessible microstates (\cref{eq:boltzmann-entropy}), which is why a system left to itself drifts towards the macrostate realised by the overwhelmingly largest number of microstates.

        A process is \emph{reversible} if it can be run backwards through the same sequence of equilibrium states leaving no net change in either the system or its surroundings---an idealisation that requires it to be infinitely slow (quasi-static) and free of friction or other dissipation. Every real process is \emph{irreversible}: it generates entropy.

    \subsection{Temperature and absolute zero}
    \label{sec:temperature}

        Thermodynamically, temperature is defined not by how much energy a system holds but by how reluctantly it accepts more,

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:temperature}
            \frac{1}{T} \equiv \left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial U}\right)_{V,N},
        \end{empheq}
        \keyeqentry{Thermodynamic temperature}

        so a cold body is one whose entropy rises steeply for a little energy added and a hot body one that barely responds. This is why heat flows from hot to cold: transferring energy from the reluctant body to the eager one raises the total entropy, and the flow stops when the two are equally eager. \emph{Absolute zero} is the limit $\left(\nicefrac{\partial S}{\partial U}\right)\to\infty$, in which the system sits in its ground state---its lowest available configuration---with no thermal excitation left to remove, so that its entropy is at a minimum, and zero if that ground state is unique. Systems whose energy has an upper bound, such as a set of two-state spins, can be driven past the point of maximum entropy into states where adding energy \emph{lowers} it, giving a formally negative temperature. These are hotter than any positive temperature rather than colder, and they are reached by inverting the populations from above rather than by passing through $T=0$, so the third law is untouched.

        For an ideal gas this abstract definition acquires a concrete mechanical meaning: temperature is a measure of the average translational kinetic energy of a molecule,\todo{Move? MIGRATION (deferred)---moving it would leave this subsection without its concrete example.}

        \begin{empheq}[box=\fbox]{equation}\label{eq:kinetic-temperature}
            \left\langle E_{\text{kin}} \right\rangle = \tfrac{3}{2} \kB T,
        \end{empheq}
        \keyeqentry{Kinetic energy and temperature}

        one factor of $\nicefrac{1}{2}\kB T$ for each of the three directions of motion. This is the \emph{equipartition theorem} (\cref{sec:macroscopic}), which gives $\nicefrac{1}{2}\kB T$ to every independent way a molecule has of storing energy. Note that \cref{eq:kinetic-temperature} is a property of the ideal gas rather than a definition of temperature---a solid or a photon gas stores its energy quite differently---whereas \cref{eq:temperature} holds for any system whatever.

        The molecules do not share this energy equally. At any instant their speeds are spread over the \emph{Maxwell--Boltzmann distribution}, whose shape is the product of two competing factors,

        \begin{equation}\label{eq:maxwell-boltzmann}
            f(v) \propto \underbrace{v^{2}}_{\text{more ways}} \, \underbrace{e^{-\nicefrac{mv^{2}}{2\kB T}}}_{\text{less likely}} ,
        \end{equation}

        the first counting the growing number of ways a molecule can have a given speed as that speed increases, the second the Boltzmann factor penalising the energy required. The result is a curve rising from zero, peaking at the most probable speed $\sqrt{\nicefrac{2\kB T}{m}}$, then falling away with a long tail towards high speeds. Because of that skew the mean speed and the root-mean-square speed $\sqrt{\nicefrac{3\kB T}{m}}$ both lie above the peak. Heating shifts the peak to the right and flattens the curve, and the sparse tail matters out of proportion to its size---it is the molecules there that evaporate from a liquid or carry enough energy to react.

    \subsection{The third law of thermodynamics}

        \textit{As the temperature of a system approaches absolute zero, its entropy approaches a constant value independent of every other parameter of the state.} This is the Nernst heat theorem, and for a perfect crystal the constant is zero. An equivalent form is the \emph{unattainability principle}: absolute zero cannot be reached by any finite number of steps, starting from a non-zero absolute temperature.

\section{Mechanical properties of material media: liquids, elastic media, mechanical waves}
\label{sec:mechanical-media}

    \paragraph{Liquids}

        Liquids are substances intermediate between a gas and a solid, having an \emph{easily variable shape but little compressibility}. The properties of a liquid follow from the behaviour of its molecules:

        \begin{itemize}
            \item they have freedom of movement within the volume occupied by the liquid
            \item \emph{intermolecular interactions} act between them---the residual electrostatic attractions between whole neutral molecules---and within the bulk of the liquid these pull in every direction equally and cancel
            \item the intermolecular interactions do not cancel at the interface between the liquid and another phase, as a result of which the phenomenon of surface tension occurs.
        \end{itemize}

   \paragraph{Elastic media}

        An elastic medium is one in which a deformation gives rise to internal stresses that \emph{oppose} it, so that the body returns to its original shape once the external force is removed. Why the response is linear follows from a general argument. A system in stable equilibrium sits at a minimum of its potential energy, any smooth minimum looks like a parabola close up (Taylor expansion), and a parabolic potential gives a force proportional to the displacement. Small disturbances therefore meet a linear elastic response whatever the medium does under large ones.

        What differs between media is which kind of deformation they resist. Solids stay elastic over a wide range of stresses and resist both a change of \emph{volume} and a change of \emph{shape}. Fluids---liquids and gases alike---always resist a change of volume, so they possess bulk elasticity, but a fluid at rest offers no resistance whatever to a change of shape, having no static shear elasticity, which is precisely what allows it to flow. Compressing the air in a cylinder closed by a piston raises its pressure and it pushes back: that is bulk elasticity, and it is all that a mechanical wave requires.\footnote{Air in a balloon is the more familiar illustration but a worse one, since the rubber skin is itself elastic and contributes a restoring force of its own, which obscures where the elasticity is coming from.} A fluid in the open shows an apparent shape elasticity only under fast forcing and small displacements, when it has no time to flow out of the way, partly a result of the approximation above.

    \paragraph{Mechanical waves}

        Elasticity and inertia together are what make a wave possible. Displace a small region of an elastic medium from its equilibrium position and the restoring force pulls it back, its inertia carries it past, and it oscillates. Because the region is coupled to its neighbours the oscillation is handed on, and the disturbance travels through the medium as a whole while the medium itself only moves to and fro about a fixed point. The speed is set by the medium alone, being the square root of the ratio of its stiffness to its density, so a stiffer medium carries a wave faster and a denser one slower.

        All of this presumes the linear elastic response argued for above, and that assumption is what makes wave propagation simple. A linear restoring force gives a sinusoidal oscillation at a single frequency, waves of every amplitude travel at the same speed, and two waves meeting pass through one another and simply add, which is the principle of \emph{superposition}. The description is accordingly trustworthy for small displacements only. Under large ones the response stops being linear, the speed starts to depend on the amplitude, and a sound wave steepens into a shock front instead of travelling undistorted.

        Waves are classified by the direction in which the medium oscillates relative to the direction of travel, which is also the direction in which energy is carried, and the classification follows directly from which kind of elasticity is available.

        A wave is \emph{longitudinal} when the medium oscillates parallel to the direction of propagation. It needs only bulk elasticity, so it can travel through anything. Sound in air is the standard example, as is a spring alternately compressed and extended.

        A wave is \emph{transverse} when the medium oscillates perpendicular to the direction of propagation. It needs shear elasticity and so travels through solids only, which is how seismology distinguishes the Earth's liquid outer core from the solid mantle. Examples are a plucked string and a vibrating ruler.
